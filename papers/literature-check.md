## Summary: published theory outruns the trial arithmetic
Most of the project's theory is already in print, while its specific numbers about what dog trials can detect were not found in the searches run. The escape-velocity threshold, the Gompertz life-expectancy formula with its "cutting death rates adds surprisingly little" result, and the senescent-cell (saturating-removal) model fitted to dog breeds from the same UK data the project uses are all published, the last one in *Nature Aging* in June 2026. The general ideas behind the trial experiments are also published: that a treatment can either lower the level of death risk or slow the rate at which risk climbs, that the two are hard to tell apart, and that stopping a treatment and watching what happens is the classic way to separate them. What the searches did not turn up is any published calculation of how often a dog or human trial would recognise a slowed rate of aging, any published error rate for a fading benefit being misread as faster aging, or any published frailty or between-breed variance figure for dogs. As of 10 November 2026 no dog longevity trial has reported a lifespan result and no longevity drug for dogs is approved: the rapamycin trial (TRIAD) had 210 of its planned 580 dogs at the last published count and is scheduled to end in November 2029, and Loyal's 1,300-dog STAY study finished enrolling in July 2025 with no results and no public power statement. TRIAD's own claim of "greater than 80% power" does not contradict the project's figure of about 18%, because the two describe different effect sizes and different follow-up; a rough check in this report using TRIAD's own published numbers gives about 29% power for a 20% risk cut over three years. "Not found" here means not found in web and abstract searches in which many key papers could only be read as abstracts, so it is a statement about this search, not about the whole literature.

## Rate versus level: a 25-year-old distinction with no published trial power number

The Gompertz law says that an adult's chance of dying rises by a roughly fixed percentage each year. It has two numbers. The **level** (also called the intercept, baseline or "vulnerability") sets how high the risk is to begin with. The **slope** (often called the "rate of aging") sets how fast risk climbs, and is usually reported as a doubling time: the years it takes for death risk to double. A treatment that lowers the level cuts risk by the same proportion at every age and acts at once; a treatment that lowers the slope does little at first and more with each passing year. Scientists call the momentary risk of death the *hazard*, and the ratio of treated to untreated risk the *hazard ratio*; a level effect is a constant hazard ratio.

This distinction is long established. The dominant finding in mice is that most genetic and drug interventions, including rapamycin, change the level and not the slope. A pooled analysis of 48 studies found that in mice reduced mTOR signalling (the pathway rapamycin acts on) lowered the level, while its effect on the slope was indistinguishable from zero (estimate −0.016, with a plausible range from −0.10 to +0.07), whereas dietary restriction lowered the slope ([Garratt et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4933670/)) [full text]. Reviews of mouse genetics reach the same verdict: "life-span-extending mutations systematically fail to affect the age-dependent acceleration of mortality" ([Hughes & Hekimi 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5105868/)) [full text], and only 7 of 54 genes showed a significant slope effect ([de Magalhães et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5887152/)) [full text]. The plain-language core of the project's argument is also in print: "When DR affects vulnerability, this lowers mortality instantly, whereas a change in aging rate will gradually lower mortality risk over time" ([Simons et al. 2013](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/files/6795368/2013AgingCellSimons.pdf)) [full text].

**Published work also says slope estimates are noisy and that ordinary experiments are too small to pin them down, but mostly in words.** The explicit numbers found are few. One methods paper recommends "at least 100-500 individuals per cohort per treatment" to estimate age-specific death rates ([Pletcher 1999](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1046/j.1420-9101.1999.00058.x)) [abstract only], and a companion paper says standard methods would need "sample sizes on the order of tens of thousands" to remove sampling bias ([Promislow et al. 1999](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1046/j.1420-9101.1999.00037.x)) [abstract only]. A physics-style argument holds that separating the two Gompertz numbers needs a far larger group than measuring average lifespan, because many slope-and-level pairs fit the same data almost equally well ([Tarkhov et al. 2017](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/064477v3.full)) [abstract read directly; the body-text scaling claim is an extract]. Real examples point the same way: a 28% lengthening of the doubling time in 26 mutant mice was not statistically significant (P = 0.30) ([de Magalhães et al. 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1448866/)) [full text], and a 19% lower slope with 98 treated and 22 control mice "did not quite reach statistical significance" ([bioRxiv 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.04.22.649947v1.full)) [extract; a preprint, meaning not yet peer reviewed]. These are in the same range as the project's "20% slowing" and agree in direction with its low detection rates, but they are anecdotes and not power calculations. (*Power* is the chance that a trial detects an effect that is really there; 80% is the usual target.)

Two published results are the mirror image of the project's and should be cited beside it. In worms followed until all had died, a lifespan gain produced by lowering the slope was just as easy to detect as one produced by lowering the level, and a 12% lifespan gain needed 150 animals per group for 80% power ([Petrascheck & Miller 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/genetics/articles/10.3389/fgene.2017.00092/full)) [full text]. So detecting *that* lifespan changed is not the hard part; saying *which* number changed is, and no paper found puts a figure on that. And for rodent studies cut short at two years, "truncated studies with roughly 3.4 times as many rodents will often have equivalent or greater power" ([Robertson et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21051569/)) [abstract only]: for ordinary effects, more animals can substitute for more time. The project's claim is that this substitution largely fails for slope effects (5,000 dogs for 3 years giving only 33%). That specific contrast was not found in the searches run.

**No published power analysis for detecting a slope change in a companion-dog trial or a human trial was found in the searches run.** The arithmetic behind the project's result is simple and worth showing. Using the slope that TRIAD itself published (0.29 per year, a doubling time of about 2.4 years), a treatment that cut the slope by 20% from the day of enrolment would lower death risk by only about 6% after one year, 16% after three years and 21% after four, but 44% after ten (the researcher's arithmetic from TRIAD's published slope, not a figure in any paper) ([Coleman et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12181551/)). A three- or four-year window sees only the weak early part of such an effect.

One caution applies to the project's wording. Whether the Gompertz slope really measures "the rate of aging" is disputed. A 2026 worm study argues the reverse of the traditional reading: that a lower slope reflects a longer period of late-life decrepitude in some individuals, "not decelerated ageing" ([Zhang & Gems 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-71780-7)) [full text]. The safest phrase is "demographic rate of aging (Gompertz slope)". The limits of this part of the search: the full texts of Pletcher 1999, Promislow 1999, Shouman & Witten 1995 and Robertson 2011 could not be opened, and the first two are the most likely places for a published slope power table; conference abstracts, theses and non-English work were not searched.

## Stop-and-watch designs are standard; the "fading looks like faster aging" trap was not found stated

A stop-and-watch (treatment-withdrawal or "switch") design gives a treatment, stops it, and compares death rates afterwards. The founding experiment used 7,492 fruit flies switched between restricted and full diets. It set out the two possibilities the project uses: an intervention either slows "the accumulation of the irreversible damage that is characteristic of aging" or reduces "short-term vulnerability to death (risk)". Flies switched to a restricted diet reached the death rate of lifelong-restricted flies within 48 hours, so in flies the diet works purely on short-term risk ([Mair et al. 2003](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2003/mair2003a.pdf)) [full text; specific numbers are an extract]. The project's vocabulary and the logic of its withdrawal experiments are therefore established and should be credited to this paper.

Later switch studies show that three different things can happen after stopping, and a withdrawal analysis has to allow for all of them. The benefit can vanish completely, as in Mair's flies. Part of it can last: in 800 female mice, those returned to full feeding after long restriction kept a death rate "below that of chronic AL-fed mice", while mice first restricted at 24 months showed "no significant improvement in mortality" for seven months ([Hahn et al. 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6861129/fullTextXML)) [full text]. Or risk can rebound above the untreated level: in more than 66,000 flies, returning to a rich diet after long restriction produced "3.7 times higher hazard" than in flies always on the rich diet, peaking within 48 hours and gone after 8 days ([McCracken et al. 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7034997/fullTextXML)) [full text]. That rebound is a real "worse after stopping" result, not a statistical illusion. Hahn's team also show how hard the measurement is: they sized the study for more than 95% power to see a full reversal over six months, yet wrote that "even with this large group of mice, conclusions on age-specific mortality are reliable only in the first six to nine months post-switch".

**Evidence that a short course of rapamycin leaves a lasting benefit exists in mice, but it is small and inconsistent between the sexes.** Three months of rapamycin in middle-aged mice raised remaining life expectancy after treatment by 60% in males (P = 0.02, with 17 and 18 mice per group) and not at all in females, which the authors summarised as "persistent effects that can robustly delay aging" ([Bitto et al. 2016](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4996648/fullTextXML)) [full text]. In the larger US government testing programme, three months of rapamycin then stopping raised median male lifespan about as much as continuous treatment (9–11%), but in females gave "a mere 4% increase in median survival (p = 0.15)" against 15% for continuous treatment ([Strong et al. 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7681050/fullTextXML)) [full text]. All of these studies compare survival after treatment with standard tests; none examines the shape of the death-rate curve after stopping. The literature thus already holds both readings of the same drug: "delays aging" from the withdrawal studies and "changes level, not slope" from the pooled analysis in the previous section. That is the nearest published situation to the project's point that a lasting risk cut and genuinely slowed aging cannot be told apart after withdrawal. An explicit statement of that point was not found in the searches run.

**The project's central warning — that a benefit which fades after stopping makes a slope test report faster aging — was not found stated anywhere, with or without error rates.** The closest published case is striking, though. Fitting Gompertz curves to four mouse studies of senolytics (drugs that kill worn-out "senescent" cells), one paper found that "in all studies a senolytic treatment always led to a drop of parameter A but an increase of parameter G" (lower level, steeper slope), and interpreted the steeper slope as real: the benefit "comes at the cost of an increased actuarial ageing rate" ([Kowald & Kirkwood 2021](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.04.24.441236.full.pdf)) [preprint read in full; figures are an extract]. That paper does not consider that a fading benefit, or the built-in trade-off between fitted level and slope, could produce the same pattern without any change in aging. It is the best example for the project to cite of the reading it warns against, with the caveat that whether dosing in those four studies stopped or continued was not verified.

The project's remedy, comparing the on-treatment period with the after-treatment period, is standard practice in human medicine under the name *legacy effect*: a benefit that persists or appears after trial treatment ends. The rule there is that "to estimate legacy effects, we should instead focus on outcomes observed during the post-trial period", because curves covering the whole period carry the on-treatment benefit forward and exaggerate what lasted; in eight statin trials the after-trial benefit was real but smaller than the on-treatment benefit (hazard ratio 0.90 for death from any cause) ([Nayak et al. 2018](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6173243/fullTextXML)) [full text]. A simulation study found that the common approach of combining trial and post-trial data "gave the most biased estimates" ([Zhu et al. 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31128221)) [abstract only]. Neurology has formal two-period withdrawal designs to tell a "disease-modifying" effect from a "symptomatic" one ([McDermott et al. 2002](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12505242)) [abstract only]. What was not found is this approach applied, with error rates, to an aging-drug withdrawal trial. One published pitfall applies directly: the dogs alive after the treatment year are survivors, and the treated survivors are a slightly different mix from the placebo survivors, so "period specific HRs are subject to selection bias, even in randomised trials" and a wholly beneficial treatment can show a late hazard ratio above 1 ([Bartlett et al. 2020](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/144609/1/The%20hazards%20of%20period%20specific%20and%20weighted%20hazard%20ratios.pdf)) [full text]. Limits of this part of the search: health-economics work on "treatment effect waning", drug-modelling work on offset versus slope effects, and actuarial literature were not searched, and a statement of the fading-slope trap could sit in any of them.

## The dog trials: no results, no approved drug, and a power claim built on a level effect

### TRIAD is running, under-enrolled at last count, and due to end in November 2029

TRIAD (Test of Rapamycin In Aging Dogs) is a randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled trial, meaning dogs are assigned by chance to drug or dummy pill and neither owners nor vets know which. Its design paper [full text] plans **580 dogs (290 per group)**, aged at least 7 years, weighing 20–50 kg, neutered and healthy, given weekly oral rapamycin or placebo "for 12 months, with a subsequent 24-month post-treatment monitoring period, for a total study duration of 36 months". The primary outcome is "survival time from randomization, assessed at 3 years", analysed with a Cox model (a standard method that estimates one hazard ratio for the whole follow-up); heart, cognitive and physical-function measures are secondary ([Coleman et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12181551/)). TRIAD is therefore itself a stop-and-watch trial: one year on, two years off. Sources conflict on the weight limit: the paper says 20–50 kg, while the project's help page says "between 44 and 120 pounds", about 54 kg ([Dog Aging Project help centre](https://dogagingproject.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/1500001690382-What-is-the-TRIAD-Cohort)) [extract].

Enrolment has been slow. Published counts run from 158 dogs in the design paper (written 2024), to 170 in December 2024, "over 180" in February 2025 ([AVMA News](https://www.avma.org/news/7m-grant-rescues-dog-study-investigating-rapamycin-canine-aging)) [extract], and **210 in a review published 19 September 2025** ([Ryave & Coleman 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12520851/)) [full text]. A university page last updated in December 2025 says "Enrollment is ongoing" ([University of Georgia](https://vet.uga.edu/?p=44394)) [extract]. **No count dated 2026 was found, so current enrolment is not verified.** The trial nearly lost its funding in 2024 and was rescued by a five-year, $7 million federal grant; the federal grants database shows the second year was paid ($2,636,580 for December 2025 to November 2026) and the grant runs to 30 November 2029 ([NIH RePORTER](https://reporter.nih.gov/project-details/11238892)). The wider Dog Aging Project also won roughly $29 million of renewed federal support, announced 27 October 2026, though that announcement does not mention TRIAD ([Texas A&M](https://stories.tamu.edu/news/2026/10/27/dog-aging-project-expands-search-for-clues-to-healthier-aging/)) [extract].

**No TRIAD results exist.** The project's own summary of 22 December 2025 says "The TRIAD study is still in progress" and "we don't have scientific results yet" ([Dog Aging Project](https://content.dogagingproject.org/?p=3918)) [extract], data are embargoed "until the completion of the trial", and a literature-database search on 10 November 2026 returned only the design paper ([Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/search?query=ABSTRACT%3A%22TRIAD%22%20AND%20ABSTRACT%3A%22rapamycin%22%20AND%20%28dogs%20OR%20canine%29)). The first planned early look at the data needs 200 dogs to have finished three years or died; with about 210 enrolled by 2025, that look is unlikely before about 2028 (an inference from the counts, not a statement by the trial). The two small earlier studies do not settle anything: a 10-week study of 24 dogs suggested better heart function ([Urfer et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411365/)) [abstract only], and a 6-month study of 17 dogs found "no statistically significant differences in echocardiographic parameters" ([Barnett et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10233048/)) [abstract only]. Neither measured lifespan.

### TRIAD's ">80% power" and the project's 18% describe different effects

The design paper's power statement reads: "the TRIAD randomized clinical trial will have greater than 80% power to detect as small as a 6% difference in mean total lifespan, with greater than 80% power to detect an 11% difference in survival within the 3-year study timeframe. Mortality and survival analysis predicts that approximately one-third of dogs in the placebo group will die within 3 years of enrollment." Its assumptions are stated: death data from 855 dogs gave a Gompertz level of 0.0066 and slope of 0.29; half the dogs enter at age 7–8, a quarter at 8–9 and a quarter at 9–10; and, decisively, "We assumed that the intervention with rapamycin improves life expectancy by reducing the baseline Gompertz hazard rate" ([Coleman et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12181551/)) [full text]. In plain terms, **TRIAD's power calculation assumes a level effect that lasts, with the slope untouched.** It says nothing about power if rapamycin changed the slope instead, or if the benefit faded after the 12 months of dosing. A full-text search of the paper found no occurrence of "slope", "rate of aging" or "aging rate" outside the definition of the Gompertz formula, and no planned test of a slope effect.

The paper gives two detectable effects without saying how they relate, and the one usually quoted (6% of lifespan) is not the one that matches the three-year analysis. To put TRIAD's statement and the project's on the same footing, this report ran a rough simulation using only TRIAD's published level, slope, age mix and group size, with a standard two-sided test at the 5% level and no early looks. **This is this report's own check, not a published result, and each figure is uncertain by a point or two.** It was reproduced afterwards by `interventions/triad_published_inputs.py`.

| Assumed effect (290 + 290 dogs, TRIAD's published parameters) | Result of this report's check |
|---|---|
| No treatment (placebo only) | About 30% die within 3 years, matching the paper's "approximately one-third" |
| 20% risk cut lasting all 3 years, deaths counted for 3 years | About 29% power; average total lifespan about 4% longer |
| 30% risk cut, 3 years | About 57% power; average total lifespan about 6.6% longer |
| 38% risk cut, 3 years | About 80% power; survival at 3 years about 10 percentage points higher |
| 22% risk cut, every dog followed until death | About 80% power |
| 20% risk cut during the dosing year only, 3 years | About 6% power |
| 20% slope cut from enrolment, 3 years (any difference detected) | About 8% power |

Read this way, the paper's two figures fit together under one interpretation: the "11% difference in survival" matches the three-year trial if it means about 11 percentage points more dogs alive at three years, which takes roughly a 40% cut in death risk; the "6% difference in mean total lifespan" reaches 80% power only if dogs are followed for much longer than three years, ideally to death. The paper does not state this, so it is an interpretation. On it, **the project's result does not contradict TRIAD's**: a 20% risk cut is simply a much smaller effect than the one TRIAD's three-year analysis is sized for. The project's 18% and this check's 29% for the same nominal scenario agree that power is far below 80%; the gap between them most likely reflects different assumptions about how many dogs die in three years, which this report could not inspect. The project's phrase "if death were the only endpoint" is fair, since TRIAD has secondary health measures, but death is the trial's primary endpoint and the only one with a published power figure.

A detail the project should use: the programme's original federally funded plan was "300 healthy, middle-aged" dogs "randomized into either placebo, transient (6 months) or continuous (3 years) treatment groups" ([NIH RePORTER, grant U19AG057377 Project 4](https://api.reporter.nih.gov/v2/projects/search)) [extract]. The dropped continuous arm is the one that would have let the trial itself separate "works only while given" from "lasting". No source states why the design changed, and no statement by the designers about what they expect after dosing stops was found.

### Loyal has three partial FDA acceptances, no approval and no public analysis plan

**As of 10 November 2026 no Loyal drug is approved, conditionally or otherwise.** This rests on Loyal's own pages and on press dated up to 21 October 2026; two FDA pages could not be opened, so nothing here is verified on the FDA's own site, and the FDA does not publish the letters involved. Conditional approval is a US pathway that lets an animal drug be sold for up to five years, renewed annually, once safety and manufacturing are established and there is a "reasonable expectation of effectiveness", with full proof of effectiveness to follow. An application has three major sections: effectiveness, safety and manufacturing.

LOY-002, a daily tablet for dogs aged 10 or older weighing at least 14 pounds, is furthest along: Loyal says the effectiveness section was accepted in February 2025 and the safety section in December 2025 (announced 13 January 2026), leaving manufacturing, which the company "expects to complete... and apply for XCA next year", that is in 2027 ([Loyal, 13 January 2026](https://loyal.com/posts/loy-002-tas-announcement)) [extract; company statement]; trade press confirms "2 of 3 major technical sections" ([DVM360, 13 January 2026](https://www.dvm360.com/view/lifespan-extension-drug-in-development-for-senior-dogs-reaches-a-new-milestone)) [extract]. LOY-001, an injection for large dogs aged 7 or older, has had only its effectiveness section accepted, in November 2023 ([Loyal, 28 November 2023](https://loyal.com/posts/loyal-announces-historic-fda-milestone-for-large-dog-lifespan-extension-drug)) [extract; company statement]. LOY-003, a daily pill for large dogs, reached the same point on 16 September 2026 ([BioSpace, 16 September 2026](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/loyal-earns-third-efficacy-acceptance-rxe-from-the-fda-for-canine-lifespan-extension)) [extract; company press release]. Loyal's site still says "FDA approval not guaranteed" and offers no product ([Loyal](https://loyal.com/for-dog-owners)) [extract]. Earlier company forecasts of a launch in early 2025 and then of completion by the end of 2025 were both missed, so the 2027 expectation deserves the same caution. All three effectiveness acceptances rest on indirect and mechanistic evidence; none rests on a demonstrated lifespan gain in pet dogs. The active ingredients are not disclosed in the sources opened.

The **STAY study** of LOY-002 is the only randomized lifespan evidence Loyal will have. It dosed its first dog in December 2023, reached 1,000 dogs by April 2025, was expanded, and "completed patient enrollment with 1300 dogs at 70 veterinary clinics across the US in July 2025" ([DVM360, 13 January 2026](https://www.dvm360.com/view/lifespan-extension-drug-in-development-for-senior-dogs-reaches-a-new-milestone)) [extract]. It is double-blinded and placebo-controlled, each dog is followed for about four years, and "statisticians will compare lifespan (as well as frailty and quality of life) between the two groups of dogs" ([Loyal, 9 October 2024](https://loyal.com/posts/stay-update-dr-ellen)) [extract; company statement]. **No source opened names a primary endpoint, an analysis method or a power figure.** The only numerical design claim is journalistic: the study is "designed to detect at least a one-year difference between the treated and placebo groups at minimum" ([Fortune, 11 April 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/04/11/dog-lifespan-longevity-loyal-drug-fda/)) [extract]. No STAY design paper and no efficacy result has been published. With the last dog enrolled in July 2025 and about four years of follow-up each, a final lifespan result before 2028–2029 is unlikely (an inference; Loyal has published no date). For the project's simulations, the relevant facts are that STAY enrols dogs at 10 or older, of nearly all sizes, and appears to dose continuously; that differs from TRIAD on every count.

### Other trials are small, short or aimed at disease, and none plans a slope analysis

Apart from TRIAD and STAY, no pet-dog trial with lifespan as its primary endpoint was found. A 70-dog trial of a senolytic combined with a vitamin-B3-related compound reported better owner-rated cognition at three months, though "all groups showed improvement... suggesting placebo effect" and in-clinic cognitive tests showed no difference ([Simon et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11137034/)) [abstract only]. A cancer-prevention vaccine trial enrolled about 800 dogs for five years and ended in May 2024, with cancer incidence as its primary outcome and death from any cause as a secondary one ([Burton et al. 2023](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/38056066)) [abstract only]; its efficacy analysis was described as "still ongoing" in June 2024 ([Calviri](https://www.azbio.org/calviri-announces-completion-of-groundbreaking-preventative-canine-cancer-vaccine-trial)) [extract; company statement], and no efficacy paper was found, so its current status is not verified. A preprint reports that in 99 retired sled dogs, half given the antiviral lamivudine for about 30 months, lifespan was not significantly changed and effects on a biological-age marker "were reversible upon treatment discontinuation" ([Matrenok et al. 2026](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.02.10.705136v1.full.pdf)) [extract; preprint]. Two gene-therapy efforts, a 17-dog heart-valve pilot and a 24-beagle laboratory study, rest on company statements relayed by news sites and have no lifespan endpoint. The Dog Aging Project's observational cohort is far larger than any trial: "50,188 companion dogs... and 14,978 documented deaths" as of 31 December 2024 ([Keyser et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-026-02580-6)) [abstract only], with a first survival analysis of 41,047 dogs published 27 October 2026 ([Chiu et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-026-02448-9)) [abstract only].

No public document from any of these trials commits to testing whether treatment changes the slope. The full analysis plans of TRIAD and STAY are not public, so an unannounced slope analysis cannot be ruled out. Other gaps: no trial registry was searched directly, and smaller registered studies may exist.

## Dog mortality models: the project's slopes reproduce, its late-life and frailty numbers stand alone

**No peer-reviewed paper was found that fits a Gompertz curve to either of the two tables the project used**, the UK table ([Teng et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9050668/)) and the US table ([Montoya et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fvets.2023.1082102/full)); both papers publish death rates by age and stop there [full text]. The project's slopes of about 0.32 per year (UK) and 0.34 (US) are nonetheless reproducible. A researcher re-fitting the printed rates got 0.30–0.35 for the UK and 0.30–0.36 for the US depending on which ages were included, so the answer moves by about ±0.03 with the age window; a Japanese insurance table gave about 0.40 (the researcher's calculation from published tables, not a published value). The closest published figures are TRIAD's slope of **0.29 per year** from 855 large dogs, a doubling time of about 2.4 years ([Coleman et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12181551/)) [full text]; a reference database's species entry giving a doubling time of **3 years**, with no source or age range stated ([AnAge](https://genomics.senescence.info/species/entry.php?species=Canis_familiaris)) [extract]; and growth rates of "0.29-0.36 years^-1" for late-life diagnoses in US veterinary records, which the authors call consistent with dog mortality slopes ([Salt et al. 2023](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10061556)) [full text]. One published pair of dog slopes, 0.0214 and 0.0219, is printed without units and cannot be per-year values, since that would make dogs age four times more slowly than people ([Hoffman et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5946068/)) [full text]; it should not be quoted as a comparison without that warning. The slope is the project's best-supported dog number: two differently built national tables and an independent disease analysis all land between about 0.3 and 0.36.

The project's "dogs age 3.3–3.7 times faster than humans" was not found stated anywhere. It sits between two published conversions: 2.7, implied by the same database's doubling times of 3 years for dogs and 8 for humans, and 4.6 human years per dog year, obtained by lining up the ages at which cancer deaths peak ([Hoffman et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5946068/)) [full text]. The familiar "multiply by seven" is described by the Dog Aging Project's founders as roughly approximating "the difference in rates of aging between dogs and people" ([Kaeberlein et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4936929/)) [full text]. Ratios based on lifespan come out higher than ratios based on slope because dogs also start from a much higher level of risk.

**That dog death risk climbs more slowly at very old ages is published in words; the size of the gap is not.** The 2026 *Nature Aging* paper, working with four breeds from the same UK data, says dogs show "a Gompertz-like exponential rise in hazard that slows at very old ages" ([Raz et al. 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-026-01138-7)) [full text], and an earlier study found a decelerating curve fitted larger breeds better ([Kraus et al. 2013](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios601/Applications/LongevityOfDogs.pdf)) [full text]. The project's specific figures (Gompertz overstating risk by 19% at age 16 and 50–95% at ages 17–19) were not found. A researcher's re-fit of the printed UK rates gave 7% at 16, 33% at 17, 59% at 18 and 69% at 19: the same direction and order of size, but different numbers, which shows how much the result depends on fitting choices. This is the project number most open to challenge, for three reasons the sources themselves supply.

The first is how the UK table was built. It contains only dogs that died; the number "alive" at each age is just the count of recorded deaths at that age or later, with no living population behind it. The authors say such tables "are more susceptible to the influence of population instability, which is common in dogs due to sudden and dramatic fad-like changes in breed popularity" ([Teng et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9050668/)). The second is thin data: only 78 deaths at age 19–20 and 23 beyond, in records where owners are known to round old dogs' ages. The third is that the three tables disagree about where the bend starts: about 16 in the UK, about 13–14 in the US table (which pools everything above 17 because "Data were sparse"), and not until 17–18 in the Japanese table. A real biological slowdown should appear at a similar age in all three.

**No frailty model fitted to dogs, and no frailty variance for dogs, was found in the searches run.** "Frailty" in this statistical sense means hidden differences between individuals in how likely they are to die; as the frailest die first, the survivors look sturdier and the population's death rate bends over. The 2013 study raises exactly this idea for large breeds and then explains that it did not fit such models because they need too many parameters ([Kraus et al. 2013](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios601/Applications/LongevityOfDogs.pdf)). The project's variance of about 0.10, fitted to an all-dog table, cannot separate true individual differences from the simple fact that the table mixes short-lived and long-lived breeds, or from the deaths-only construction. Likewise no published between-breed variance of death risk was found; published ranges (hazard ratios from 0.5 to 3.0 across 155 UK breeds in [McMillan et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-50458-w) [full text], and a 6.8-fold range of death rates in Swedish insured dogs) are compatible with the project's 0.15–0.7 band only as a back-of-envelope reading. The project's sensitivity to outlier breeds matches the published picture: the UK table's French Bulldog life expectancy of 4.53 years is flagged by its own authors as biased by "recent soaring popularity", and a study that accounts for dogs still alive gives a median of 9.8 years for the same breed ([McMillan et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-50458-w)).

On why large dogs die younger, the project's hedge between "risk starts higher" and "risk rises faster" sits between published positions that disagree. The best-known paper concludes "large dogs die young mainly because they age quickly" ([Kraus et al. 2013](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios601/Applications/LongevityOfDogs.pdf)), but that headline rests on the absolute yearly increase in risk; its own measure of the *proportional* yearly increase, which is what a Gompertz slope is, shows a weak size effect that disappears at older ages. A follow-up found a small but significant size effect on the Gompertz slope ([Yordy et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7326369/)) [full text], and a third attributes the size effect to a higher minimum death rate "and thus higher age-dependent mortality throughout adult life", a level description ([da Silva & Cross 2023](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37229711)) [abstract only]. All three hazard-fitting papers use one US teaching-hospital dataset with only five adult age bands. Gaps: the per-breed fitted values in these papers' supplements were not opened, and several older dog-mortality papers could not be retrieved.

## Escape velocity, Gompertz life expectancy and the dog senescent-cell fit are all in print

**The escape-velocity threshold is published, in words in 2004 and as a formula in 2011.** De Grey asked what happens if "mortality rates fall so fast that people's remaining (not merely total) life expectancy increases with time" and answered that the required rate "is simply the ratio of the mortality rates at consecutive ages... which is only about 10% per year", naming it "actuarial escape velocity" ([de Grey 2004](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC423155/)) [full text]. The demographic formula appears in a 2011 paper: if death rates at every age fall by a fraction ρ a year, a person's own risk rises with age at the Gompertz slope *minus* ρ, which reaches zero exactly at the project's threshold ([Missov & Lenart 2011](https://www.demographic-research.org/volumes/vol24/19/24-19.pdf)) [full text], and a 2006 paper states that its life-expectancy formula holds only while the yearly decline stays below the slope ([Goldstein & Wachter 2006](https://u.demog.berkeley.edu/~jrw/Biblio/Eprints/%20G-I/goldstein.wachter.2006_GapsLags_PopStudies.pdf)) [full text]. The project's "about 8% a year" and de Grey's "about 10%" both sit inside the published range of human slopes, 0.06–0.15 per year ([Missov et al. 2015](https://www.demographic-research.org/volumes/vol32/36/32-36.pdf)) [full text]. Only the project's side-by-side pairing of the threshold with an observed improvement of 0.5–1% a year was not found in one place; de Grey contrasts it with "more than 2% per year", and the 2006 paper's worked example uses 1%. An earlier result usually credited for this line of work (Vaupel 1986) is known here only second-hand [not verified].

**The Gompertz life-expectancy formula and the "small gains" result are published.** The approximation the project uses, life expectancy ≈ (1/slope) × (ln(slope/level) − 0.577), is equation 9 of a 2013 paper ([Missov 2013](https://www.demographic-research.org/volumes/vol28/9/28-9.pdf)) [full text], and an older attribution to Pollard and Valkovics is cited elsewhere [not verified]. The observation that removing a large share of deaths adds few years is the *Taeuber paradox*: eliminating cancer, then 18% of deaths, "amounted to 2.265 years, or 3 percent" of life expectancy for US men ([Keyfitz 1977](https://gwern.net/doc/longevity/1977-keyfitz.pdf)) [full text]. The project's "halving all death rates adds about 7–8 years" follows in one line from the 2013 formula (a derivation by the researcher, not a quoted sentence) and gives 6.0–11.4 years across published human slopes, bracketing the project's figure. By contrast, **no citable source was found for the "freeze risk at its age-30 level" lifespan of hundreds to over 1,000 years**; the only numerical no-aging lifespan located is a blog calculation from injury deaths alone, about 1,200 years for the US ([Biostasis](https://biostasis.substack.com/p/how-long-might-we-live-if-aging-were)) [extract]. This figure is widely attributed to books that are not online, so the gap is probably one of access.

**The saturating-removal model fitted to dogs is published, using the same UK data.** The model treats aging as damage (originally, senescent cells) that is produced faster with age and removed by a clearing system that saturates, that is, cannot speed up beyond a ceiling; death comes when damage crosses a threshold. It was introduced in 2019 with mouse and human parameters ([Karin et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6889273)) [full text]. A June 2026 paper fitted it to nine species including four UK dog breeds from Teng et al. — Labrador, German Shepherd, Staffordshire Bull Terrier and Jack Russell, each with over 1,000 dogs — and concluded that "the production rate η, is the best single predictor of lifespan differences between species" ([Raz et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13278975/)) [full text; supplement not opened]. From its tables, the Labrador's fitted damage-production rate is about 3.4 times a human man's while the removal capacity is nearly the same, though the Labrador estimate is loose, with a plausible range spanning more than a factor of three (the researcher's arithmetic from the paper's tables). The authors add cautions the project should adopt: the parameters "are mathematical constructs rather than directly measured biological quantities", different parameter combinations fit almost equally well, and "one needs at least n = 500 individuals to obtain errors smaller than twofold". What that paper's main text does not give is a comparison between dog breeds; per-breed values are in a supplement that was not opened, so whether the project's breed-level results match is unknown.

Two smaller items complete the picture. That hidden frailty bends a Gompertz curve into a levelling-off curve is credited to Beard (1959) and to Vaupel, Manton and Stallard (1979) ([Missov et al. 2015](https://www.demographic-research.org/volumes/vol32/36/32-36.pdf)) [attribution read in full text; the originals and the plateau formula not verified]. For periodic repair, the published results are computer scans, not a formula: for example, treatment "as infrequent as once per 2 months, that kills only 40% of the drug-sensitive senescent cells, shifts the incidence curve back by 10 years" ([Katzir et al. 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7963340/fullTextXML)) [full text]. A closed-form condition on treatment interval and fraction removed was not found in the searches run, but the most likely home for one, a 57-page 2007 paper by Phoenix and de Grey, could be read only as an abstract.

## Conclusion

The project's distinctive contribution, on this evidence, lies less in its theory than in putting numbers on a question the real trials have left open. TRIAD publishes a Gompertz slope and then holds it fixed; Loyal publishes no analysis plan at all; and the aging literature has argued for 25 years about slope versus level using finished, whole-life animal cohorts, never a three- or four-year trial that enrols in mid-life and, in TRIAD's case, stops dosing after one year. That is why no published counterpart to the detection rates was found, and also why the claim needs careful framing: the gap may reflect the limits of this search, in which the two most likely sources of a slope power table were readable only as abstracts. The strongest position for the project is to present its theory sections as a clearly cited restatement of known results, and its trial simulations as a worked application to TRIAD and STAY whose inputs anyone can check, starting with TRIAD's own published level, slope and age mix. The dog-mortality numbers divide the same way: the slope is solid and independently corroborated, while the late-life bend and the frailty variance rest on the least reliable corner of a deaths-only table.

### Concrete changes for the project

1. **Credit the escape-velocity threshold** to de Grey 2004 (verbal statement, "about 10% per year"), Missov & Lenart 2011 (formula) and Goldstein & Wachter 2006 (the same condition as a restriction), and present the project's version as a restatement.
2. **Cite Missov 2013 for the life-expectancy formula and Keyfitz 1977 for the Taeuber paradox**; label "halving adds about 7–8 years" as a one-line consequence of the published formula, and label the "freeze at age 30" lifespan as the project's own arithmetic with no citable source located.
3. **Cite Raz et al. 2026 (*Nature Aging*) prominently in the saturating-removal section**, state that it fitted the same UK breed data, compare the project's fitted values with that paper's published Labrador values, and adopt its warnings about loosely determined parameters. Open its supplement to check the breed-level results.
4. **Reword "rate of aging" to "Gompertz slope (demographic rate of aging)"** and add a sentence that the biological meaning of the slope is disputed, citing Zhang & Gems 2026.
5. **Restate the TRIAD comparison precisely.** Quote TRIAD's power statement and its assumption of a lasting level effect; say that the project's 18% concerns a smaller effect (a 20% risk cut) than TRIAD's three-year figure (an 11% difference in survival); and avoid any wording that implies TRIAD's authors miscalculated.
6. **Re-run the power calculations with TRIAD's published inputs** (level 0.0066, slope 0.29, entry ages 7–10 in the stated mix, about one-third of placebo dogs dying in three years, early looks after 200 and 400 dogs) and report results beside the current ones, stating the assumed three-year death fraction each time. Explain the difference from the roughly 29% obtained in this report's check.
7. **Correct the trial descriptions**: TRIAD's 580 dogs is a target (210 enrolled at the last published count, September 2025; later counts not verified); dosing lasts 12 months of a 36-month follow-up; STAY enrols dogs aged 10 or older of nearly all sizes, has 1,300 dogs, about four years of follow-up, and no public primary endpoint or power figure. Model a STAY-like scenario with those entry ages instead of TRIAD's.
8. **Credit the withdrawal logic and add the missing scenarios.** Cite Mair 2003 for risk versus damage, Hahn 2019 and McCracken 2020 for partial persistence and rebound, and Nayak 2018 and Zhu 2019 for on-treatment versus after-treatment analysis. Add a rebound-above-control scenario and a partly-lasting scenario to the simulations, and add the survivor-selection caveat from Bartlett 2020 to the two-period analysis.
9. **Use Kowald & Kirkwood 2021 as the worked example** of a "lower level, steeper slope" fit read as faster aging, noting that the dosing schedules in the underlying studies were not verified.
10. **Soften the late-life and frailty claims.** Report the dog slope with its sensitivity to age window (about ±0.03) and cite TRIAD's 0.29, AnAge's 3-year doubling time and Salt 2023 as comparisons. Present the overshoot after age 16 and the frailty variance of 0.10 as tentative, citing the deaths-only construction of the UK table, the 78 deaths at age 19–20, the earlier bend in the US table and the mixing of breeds. Treat the US table, which has a living population behind it, as the primary test of slope.
11. **Flag biased breeds and frame the size question as open.** Exclude or mark the French Bulldog (4.53 years in the UK table against 9.8 in a study that counts living dogs), compute between-breed variance directly from the 155-breed supplementary table in McMillan 2024, and cite Kraus 2013, Yordy 2020 and da Silva & Cross 2023 as disagreeing on whether size acts on slope or level.
12. **Replace "novel" or "first" language throughout with "not found in the searches run"**, and list the search limits: several key papers read only as abstracts, supplements unopened, FDA pages and trial analysis plans unavailable, and health-economics, actuarial and non-English literature unsearched.
