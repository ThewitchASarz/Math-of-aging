# Known gaps

Things in this project that are unverified, unresolved or approximate. Read this before relying on any number.

## Not verified against a primary source

- **Dog somatic mutation rate.** The project uses a prediction (200 to 270 substitutions per genome per year) from the inverse-lifespan relationship in Cagan et al. 2022. The literature check (`papers/literature-check.md`) read the measured value in the paper's Supplementary Table 3 as 249.5 per year, inside that range. The project's own code has not been changed to use it, and the table was read by one research agent only.
- **Share of children expected to reach 100 (Olshansky et al. 2024).** Two readings of the paper disagreed on the exact figure (5.1% or 5.3% of girls). The report quotes only the paper's stated ceiling: unlikely to exceed 15% of women and 5% of men this century.
- **TAME metformin trial status.** That it has not enrolled anyone rests on the funder's fundraising page and on news reports from September 2026, not on a trial registry.
- **Loyal LOY-002 approval status.** "No conditional approval as of 10 November 2026" is an absence of evidence in the sources found. The remaining FDA section is described as manufacturing by trade press, not by the company's own page.
- **SPRINT and EMPA-REG OUTCOME hazard ratios** (0.73 and 0.68). Taken from summary pages and not re-checked against the journal papers. SELECT (0.81) and the statin meta-analyses (0.90 in 2010; 0.88 in 2005) were checked.
- **US death rates above age 95.** The Social Security table's rates at these ages are actuarial estimates. Results about the late-life plateau and the oldest attainable age reflect those estimates.
- **Cause-of-death shares by age.** The cause-elimination calculation uses New York State 2023 shares applied to US death rates. A rough check against US national shares (NVSR vol. 74 no. 10) gave the same conclusions, but the calculation was not redone age by age.
- **Mouse single-gene results** (Ames dwarf, Snell dwarf, growth-hormone-receptor knockout, klotho, telomerase). Magnitudes were not verified and are not used in any calculation.
- **Karin et al. 2019 equation form.** The parameter values were confirmed from the paper. The exact form of the equation appears in a figure that could not be read; the form used matches the paper's text and Alon's lecture notes.
- **Frailty-index growth rates** in humans and dogs, and the **historical trend in life expectancy at age 80**: not retrieved.
- **Large-animal safety data for partial reprogramming**: none found.

## Unresolved conflicts

- **Power of the dog rapamycin trial (TRIAD): explained, with one loose end.** The design paper's "greater than 80% power" assumes a risk cut that lasts, with the rate at which risk rises left unchanged, and it is sized for a large effect. Re-running the calculation with the paper's own published inputs (`interventions/triad_published_inputs.py`) gives about 29% power for a 20% risk cut over 3 years, about 80% for a 38% cut, 7% if the cut lasts only for the dosing year, and 9% for a 20% slower rise of risk. Our earlier quoted figure of about 18% was a rough calculation. The code in this repository gives about 23% (`interventions/trial_design.py`: every dog enters at exactly age 7, so 21% of placebo dogs die in 3 years, against 30% with the paper's inputs) and 27 to 43% (experiment 001, depending on entry ages). All of these are far below 80%, and none contradicts the paper, which is sized for a larger effect. Loose end: the paper's two stated effect sizes (6% of mean lifespan; 11% in survival within 3 years) are not explained in the paper, and our reading of them is an interpretation. The trial doses for 12 months of its 36; experiments here that assume treatment throughout do not describe it.
- **Dog-to-human aging speed.** The ratio is 3.3 to 3.7 depending on the fitting method. Statements of "about 3.5" use the middle of that range.

## Calibrated or approximate, not fitted

- **Dog size-class mortality parameters** are calibrated to published life expectancies, one number per size class, because size-specific life tables were not available.
- **The within-dog scaling of lifespan with body mass** (exponent about -0.11) rests on five size-group points and assumed representative masses.
- **"Slowing aging by 10%"** adds 3 to 5 years depending on how slowing is defined.
- **Theorem 11** in the theory paper is an asymptotic approximation (within 2% at ages 40 to 60 for the published human parameters). The other theorems are exact within their stated assumptions.

## Found by the experiments

- **The frozen-production shortcut for the worn-out-cell model is poor for dogs** (`experiments/002_dog_cell_model/sde_check.py`; experiment 005). At dog-like settings it differs from the model solved directly by up to 15 to 32 percent. Experiment 005 refitted without the shortcut and experiment 002's conclusions held, so they no longer depend on it. The same shortcut is used in `damage_repair/saturating_removal.py` for humans, where experiment 005 measured its error at 3.5 percent or less in the death rate between ages 40 and 85.
- **The worn-out-cell model misses dog death rates in a fixed pattern** (experiment 005): too low at age 2 by 40 to 50 percent, too high at ages 5 to 8, too low at 13 to 16, in every fit. Most of the error comes from ages 2 to 4. The model has no deaths unrelated to aging, which is the likely cause; untested with the direct solution (queued as 025).
- **No ranking of the three two-setting explanations is supported** (experiments 002 and 005). The order by error flips on the US table when young ages are left out. "Dogs clear at about half the human rate" holds only inside the clearance fit, with every other setting held at its human value.
- **The brute-force simulation in `experiments/002_dog_cell_model/sde_check.py` reads about 4 to 6 percent low** (experiment 005). It looks for death only at the end of each half-day step. The file has been left as it was; `experiments/005_cell_model_direct/direct_fit.py` has a corrected simulation.
- **The human settings of the worn-out-cell model (Karin et al. 2019) were not re-opened on 17 November 2026** (experiment 005, by us or by the independent check). They rest on experiment 002's reading.
- **Predicted senescent-cell lifetimes in dogs were withdrawn** as lab predictions; see `experiments/002_dog_cell_model/RESULT.md`.
- **The fingerprint test is valid only while treatment continues** (experiment 003). A benefit that fades after treatment stops is misread as faster aging. No analysis for stop-and-watch trials has been built yet.
- **The doubling law overstates dog death rates from about age 16** (experiment 004): by 19 percent at 16 and by 50 to 95 percent at 17 to 19 when fitted to younger ages. Results here that concern dogs older than 15 lean on it.
- **Hidden differences between dogs.** The fingerprint test holds up under frailty of the size fitted to the UK table, but not under frailty three times larger. Differences between breeds in level of risk were tested in experiment 008 and do not break it.
- **Stop-and-watch trials: use the two-period analysis of experiment 006, and know its limits.** It cannot tell slowed aging from a risk cut that persists; a partial or slow fade is reported as lasting (tested in experiment 009); and "no lasting benefit" is not evidence against modest slowing (power 8 percent for a year of 20 percent slowing in 580 dogs).
- **Ignoring size class inflates the slope test's false-alarm rate to about 9 percent in 4-year trials** (experiment 007). A baseline per size class fixes it.
- **Breed differences in level of risk do not break the slope test** (experiment 008), at any published spread from 0.15 to 0.71. This rests on assumptions: breeds differ only in level, share one rate of aging, and every dog of a breed has the same risk. The French Bulldog figure remains unreliable, but the result does not depend on it.
- **The "spread" number is a poor yardstick for the slope test** (experiment 008). A spread of 0.3 breaks the test when it is spread across all dogs and does nothing when it sits in a few rare, short-lived breeds. Statements in experiments 004 and 007 that compare a spread with "0.3 broke the test" should be read with that in mind.
- **Hidden differences inside breeds are unmeasured.** No source for their size has been found. If they are widely spread with a spread near 0.3, the slope test gives about 22 percent false alarms for a 50 percent risk cut in a 10-year trial. Experiment 012 built an analysis that allows for them: one extra setting brings false alarms back to about 5 percent with no loss of power found, in a 1,300-dog, 10-year trial.
- **The hidden-differences analysis of experiment 012 is tested only in an ideal long trial.** Not tested: trials of 3 to 4 years, where few dogs die and the spread is hard to estimate; dropout and a mix of sizes; a spread wider than 0.3 or dogs in two distinct groups; a treatment that helps frail dogs more than sturdy ones. The spread of 0.3 is an assumption.
- **Breeds that age at different rates have not been simulated.** A baseline per breed would not repair that.
- **The trial-size result of experiment 010 was idealised; experiment 011 added dropout, bending risk and a mix of sizes.** The answer becomes a range, about 1,300 to 1,600 dogs, with about 2,000 for a margin. It still assumes perfect adherence and an effect that acts fully from enrolment.
- **Dropout rates in experiment 011 are assumptions (0, 5 and 10 percent a year).** No published dropout rate for a dog trial was found. The experiment could not open the design paper of the rapamycin dog trial (TRIAD, Coleman et al. 2025); `papers/literature-check.md` records other figures from that paper but no dropout rate.
- **Dropout linked to health or to treatment was simulated in experiment 014, with assumed patterns only.** The slope test keeps its usual false-alarm rate (5 to 7 percent) unless the share of deaths that go unrecorded drifts over time in one group (11 percent of trials for a drift to 15 percent, 29 for a drift to 30). The ordinary risk comparison is fooled more easily: sick dogs withdrawn before death in 30 percent of treated and 15 percent of untreated cases make a useless treatment look like a 17 percent risk cut in 69 percent of trials. No published dropout pattern for a dog trial was found, so whether any of this happens in practice is unknown.
- **Experiment 014 covers one design only** (1,300 dogs, 10 years, treatment throughout). Not tested: a 3- or 4-year trial, a wider hidden spread combined with frail dogs leaving, drifts of other shapes, and how much tracing of withdrawn dogs repairs the problem (queued as 024).
- **The slope test fires in 5 to 7 percent of trials, not exactly 5, when hidden differences of the fitted size are present and not modelled** (experiment 014 and its check, 2,500 to 3,000 trials). Earlier statements of "about 5 to 6 percent" stand; differences of one or two points between cases in 800-trial runs are noise.
- **Experiment 011 applies the hidden risk factor of experiment 004 inside every size class.** Part of the bend in the UK table comes from the size mix, so the bend is probably counted partly twice.
- **The UK dog table is built from deaths in one period**, not by following dogs. Its shape at the oldest ages mixes aging with breed mix and recording errors.
- **The rapamycin trial's design paper could not be opened on 17 November 2026** (experiment 016, by us or by the independent check). Its inputs in experiment 016 rest on the earlier reading recorded in `papers/literature-check.md`. Two details are weakly recorded and need a re-read: the second early look "after 400 dogs" and the stopping rule (the rule used in experiment 016 is an assumption).
- **The published rapamycin-trial inputs give about 30 percent of placebo dogs dying in 3 years**, where the paper is recorded as saying "approximately one-third". A rounding-size gap, noted so that nobody reads it as a conflict.
- **That STAY doses throughout is a reading, not a confirmed fact** ("appears to dose continuously" in the literature check). Its entry ages, endpoint and analysis are not public; experiment 016 uses two guesses for the ages.
- **The trial size of experiment 010 depends on the death-rate inputs** (experiment 016). 1,300 dogs for 10 years reach about 88 percent with this project's inputs and about 78 percent with the rapamycin trial's published ones. Which input causes the drop is untested (queued as 018).
- **A benefit that ends when dosing ends is nearly undetectable in a one-year-dosing, three-year design** (experiment 016: a halving of risk for the dosing year is detected in 16 percent of 580-dog trials). This is best case: no dropout, exact doubling law.
- **Rebound sizes in experiment 019 are assumptions.** No rebound has been reported in dogs in the sources read, and the published fly figure (3.7 times, gone in 8 days) has no agreed equivalent in a dog. The experiment shows what the design could detect, not what is likely.
- **The two-period test gives one number for the whole time after dosing** (experiment 019). It cannot tell a short large rebound from a lasting small one, and it recognises a half-lasting benefit in only about a third of 580-dog trials. A three-period version is untested (queued as 021); the trial size needed is untested (queued as 020).
- **A test over the whole trial can cancel a benefit against a rebound** (experiment 019: 5.5 percent, the chance rate, for a halving followed by six months of doubled risk). Best case: no dropout, exact doubling law, full enrolment.
- **Survivor mix was tested one way only** (experiment 019): a fixed gamma-shaped hidden factor and a treatment that helps every dog equally. The after-dosing ratio between survivors is 1.01 to 1.02 in the rapamycin design and about 1.05 for an older group. A treatment that helps frail dogs more was not simulated.
- **The three animal papers on what happens after stopping (Mair 2003, Hahn 2019, McCracken 2020) and Bartlett 2020 were not re-opened on 17 November 2026** (experiment 019, by us or by the independent check). Statements about them rest on `papers/literature-check.md`.
- **"Lasting" from the two-period test means "still there on average while the trial watched"** (experiment 009). A benefit that halves every 2 years is reported as lasting in about 65 percent of 580-dog, 3-year trials; a permanent benefit of half the size in 38 percent. The ratio the test reports depends on the length of watching (0.65 with 3 years and 0.83 with 10 for the same slow fade). Earlier wording in experiments 006, 016 and 019 that a benefit is "recognised as lasting" should be read this way.
- **No analysis here tells a fading benefit from a permanent one.** In the published rapamycin design a quick fade and a small permanent benefit give the same result (experiment 009). The check's quick look at splitting the after-dosing time in two found it needs far more deaths than 580 dogs give in 3 years (flagged in 14 percent of trials; 66 percent with 5,000 dogs). Those figures are the check's own, from 400 to 1,500 trials, and are not saved results. A proper test is queued (021, 026).
- **Fading speeds and sizes in experiment 009 are assumptions**, as are its 10-year and 5,000-dog designs. It is best case: no dropout, no hidden differences, exact doubling law (which overstates risk at the ages the 10-year design reaches), full enrolment. Harder conditions are queued as 027.
- **The rapamycin trial's design paper was not re-opened on 18 November 2026** (experiment 009, by us or by the independent check), and neither were Raz et al. 2026 or McMillan et al. 2024, so experiments 022 and 023 still wait.
- **The full re-run of every result is slow on the test server.** The test run for experiment 005 was cut off at the 30-minute limit before it finished. The limit was raised to 60 minutes and the next run passed in 43 minutes (the run before experiment 005 took 17, and earlier runs of the same suite varied from 17 to over 30). The suite grows with every experiment and needs trimming or splitting; experiment 005's script may be much slower on that server than the 3 minutes measured here, which has not been looked into.

## Judgement, not data

## Before making this repository public

- **Data reuse terms have not been checked source by source.** `DATA_SOURCES.md` lists every transcribed table and what is known about its terms. Tables from government sources and open-access journals are expected to be reusable with attribution; the others need a check.
- **Novelty: a literature search has been run, an expert review has not.** `papers/literature-check.md` records what was found. Already published: the escape-velocity threshold (de Grey 2004; Missov & Lenart 2011; Goldstein & Wachter 2006), the Gompertz life-expectancy formula and the small gain from removing causes of death (Missov 2013; Keyfitz 1977), frailty bending the mortality curve (Beard 1959; Vaupel, Manton & Stallard 1979), the worn-out-cell model fitted to dog breeds from the same UK data (Raz et al. 2026, Nature Aging), and the difference between lowering the level of risk and slowing its rise, with stop-and-watch designs to separate them (Mair et al. 2003; Simons et al. 2013). Not found in the searches run: how often a dog or human trial would recognise a slower rise of risk, error rates for a fading benefit being misread, and frailty or between-breed variance figures for dogs. "Not found" is a statement about a limited search. No theorem or finding here should be described as original without review by an expert.

## Raised by the literature check

Work suggested by `papers/literature-check.md`. Items marked **experiment** are candidates for the queue in `experiments/LOG.md` and should be taken before older queue items.

- **Done as experiment 016: the two real trials with their real designs.** The earlier findings hold. About 30 percent of placebo dogs die in the rapamycin design and about 83 percent in a STAY-like one. See `experiments/016_real_trial_designs/RESULT.md`.
- **Done as experiment 019: risk that rebounds after stopping.** Published switch experiments show three outcomes after a treatment stops: the benefit vanishes (Mair et al. 2003), part of it lasts (Hahn et al. 2019), or risk rebounds above the untreated level (McCracken et al. 2020). Experiment 019 simulated the last two in the rapamycin trial's design, and the survivor-selection problem described by Bartlett et al. 2020. A rebound can make the main test read a helpful-while-given treatment as harmful; the two-period test handles it; survivor selection is negligible in this design. See `experiments/019_rebound_after_stopping/RESULT.md`.
- **Experiment, queued as 022 and waiting on the source: compare our fitted cell-model settings with Raz et al. 2026.** Tried again in a later run on 17 November 2026 (experiment 005's run) and on 18 November 2026 (experiment 009's run) and still not opened. The paper could not be opened on 17 November 2026 (neither its journal page nor its PubMed Central copy; web pages cannot be opened in an unattended run), so the comparison was not started and no number from it was guessed. That paper fitted the same model to four UK breeds. Open its supplement and compare breed by breed. Its warning applies here: different settings fit almost equally well.
- **Experiment, queued as 023 and waiting on the source: between-breed spread from a better table.** Not attempted on 17 November 2026 for the same reason; tried on 18 November 2026 and the page could not be opened. Compute the spread of risk between breeds from the 155-breed table of McMillan et al. 2024, which counts dogs still alive, in place of the deaths-only UK table.
- **Wording.** "Rate of aging" in this repository means the Gompertz slope, the rate at which death risk rises with age. Whether that slope measures biological aging is disputed (Zhang & Gems 2026). The papers have not yet been reworded.
- **Credits not yet added inside the two papers.** The theory paper now carries a prior-work note at the top, but individual theorems are not yet cited one by one.
- **The late-life bend and the frailty figure for dogs are tentative.** The UK table counts only deaths, has 78 deaths at ages 19 to 20, and bends at a different age from the US and Japanese tables. The slope itself is better supported: two national tables, the rapamycin trial's own 0.29, and an independent disease analysis (Salt et al. 2023) all fall between about 0.29 and 0.36 per year, and our fit moves by about 0.03 with the choice of ages.
- **The lifespan with risk frozen at its age-30 level** (479 and 1,138 years here) has no published source that we could find; it is this project's arithmetic.
- **Trial facts corrected.** 580 dogs is the rapamycin trial's target; 210 were enrolled at the last published count (September 2025), and the trial is due to end in November 2029. No dog longevity drug is approved as of 10 November 2026 on the company's own pages and the trade press; the FDA's own pages could not be opened.
