# Experiment 037: result

**The trap works in both directions and does not need a strong treatment. In simulated 10-year trials of 1,300 dogs, a treatment that cuts risk by 40 percent in the sturdier half of dogs and 60 percent in the frailer half is reported as faster aging in about 20 percent of trials by the hidden-differences test and about 57 percent by the plain test, though no dog ages faster. A weaker treatment is not safe either: with an average cut of 30 percent, cuts of 42 and 18 percent are reported as slowed aging in about 28 to 32 percent of trials, and cuts of 18 and 42 percent as faster aging in about 19 percent (hidden-differences test) and 38 percent (plain test).** Two predictions were confirmed, one was half right and one was refuted.

This is a calculation under stated assumptions. It says how analyses behave on simulated dogs. It does not say that any treatment works, or that any real treatment helps frail or sturdy dogs more. The spread of hidden risk and the sizes of all the cuts are assumptions.

## The setting

Every dog has a hidden personal risk factor. The "sturdier half" is the half with lower hidden risk among dogs that entered at the same age. Cuts are written as "sturdier half and frailer half". In no case does any dog's rate of aging change. So every report of faster or slowed aging below is false.

Two analyses were used, both unchanged from experiment 017. The **plain** slope test ignores hidden differences. The **hidden-differences** test (experiment 012) has one extra setting for their spread. A sound test reports faster aging in about 2.5 percent of trials and slowed aging in about 2.5 percent.

The trial: 1,300 dogs, half treated from enrolment, entry ages 7 to 12, followed for 10 years, spread of hidden risk 0.3.

## What this means

1. **The opposite direction is a real worry too. This prediction was wrong.** We expected the hidden-differences test to be misled much less often when frail dogs are helped more. It is misled less, but not much less: 20 percent of trials report faster aging at 40 and 60, against 29 percent reporting slowed aging in the mirror case (60 and 40). At 45 and 55 the two directions are about equal (9 and 10 percent).
2. **In this direction the plain test is the worse one.** It already leans towards "faster aging" when every dog gets the same cut. A benefit that favours frail dogs pushes the same way, so the two errors add: 41 percent of trials at 45 and 55, 57 percent at 40 and 60. In experiment 033 the same lean made the plain test look safer, because there the errors cancelled.
3. **A weaker treatment is not protection in itself.** If the average cut falls and the two cuts keep the same ratio (60 and 40 becomes 36 and 24, then 24 and 16), false reports of slowed aging fall from 29 percent to about 10 and about 6. That is what P3 predicted. But a 30 percent average cut split as 42 and 18 gives about 28 to 32 percent, the same as 60 and 40. And 18 and 42 gives about as many false reports of faster aging as 40 and 60 (about 19 and 20 percent). So what matters is how unequally the benefit is shared, not only how large it is on average.
4. **The false reports of faster aging level off.** With frail dogs helped more, the hidden-differences test goes 9, 20, 29, 32 percent as the difference grows from 45-and-55 to 30-and-70. The last step is small. The check saw the same and looked at one possible cause (the fitted spread running into its lower limit); that is not the reason. The cause is not known.
5. **The plain test's lean shrinks with the cut.** With an equal cut it reports faster aging in about 21 percent of trials at 50 percent, and about 5 to 9 percent at 30 and 20 percent.

For a real trial this suggests two things. A finding that a treatment "speeds up aging" from death records alone could come from a treatment that only helps frailer dogs somewhat more, which is a plausible thing for a helpful treatment to do. And the trap of experiments 017 and 033 does not go away for a treatment of modest strength. Either finding needs support from something other than the death records. This is a statement about the mathematics, not a comment on any trial's own analysis plan, which answers its own questions.

## Independent check

A separate agent wrote its own simulation and fitting code from a description of the model, with its own random draws, before opening ours. It ran all twelve cases with both tests, 800 trials each. Its figures are in the table of predictions above. Of 48 comparisons, 47 were within 2 standard errors of ours; the other (the plain test reporting faster aging at 36 and 24: 2.2 percent against our 0.8) was at 2.06, which is what chance gives among 48. Median estimated rates agreed within 0.012 and the share of dogs dying within 0.001.

It then read our code and the functions it imports from experiments 001, 012, 017 and 033 and found no errors in the simulation of hidden risk and rank, which half gets which cut, the death times, the likelihoods, the counting or the seeds. It confirmed the two UK inputs (level 0.004967 a year and slope 0.3195 a year) against `dog_lifespan/results.json`.

## What the check changed

- **One reading of ours was withdrawn.** We had drafted: "what decides the size of the false finding is the ratio between the two halves' remaining risks, not the average cut". The check tested this with cuts of 59 and 42 percent, which leave the same ratio of remaining risks as 42 and 18. It found slowed aging reported in 24.6 percent of trials against 32.1 (about 3 standard errors apart), and a larger gap for the plain test (2.8 against 13.3 percent). So the ratio alone does not decide it. Point 3 above says only what the numbers show.
- **The 42-and-18 figure is stated as a range.** Ours was 28.5 percent and the check's 32.1; the result says about 28 to 32.
- **Point 4's explanation was tested and not supported.** The fitted spread does not run into its lower limit (1 to 2 percent of fits). The cause of the levelling off is left open.
- The check also ran the mirror case 60 and 40 itself and found 29.7 percent, in line with experiment 033's 28.7.

## Comparison with published work

`papers/literature-check.md` names no published work on how a benefit that differs between individuals affects a test for a change in the rate of aging, and none was found in the searches run for it. It credits the idea of hidden frailty, and of survivors becoming a sturdier selection over time, to Beard (1959) and Vaupel, Manton and Stallard (1979); the trap measured here is that mechanism acting on the treated group. Its worked example of a "lower level, steeper slope" fit being read as faster aging is Kowald and Kirkwood (2021); the frail-helped-more cases here give the same pattern from a different cause. No new search was run, because web pages could not be opened in this run (Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 were tried again on 19 November 2026 and could not be opened).

## Limits

1. Everything is assumed: the spread of hidden risk (0.3 has no measurement behind it), the sizes of the cuts, and the two-halves pattern. Nobody has measured whether any treatment helps frail or sturdy dogs more.
2. One spread (0.3), one trial size (1,300 dogs), one length (10 years), one size of dog, no dropout. Experiment 033 found that a narrower spread and a 4-year trial do not remove the trap for sturdy dogs helped more; neither was tested for the opposite direction or for weaker treatments.
3. Only the two-halves pattern was used. Experiment 033 found a steadily varying benefit is worse.
4. Why the false reports of faster aging level off is not known.
5. No rule was found that predicts the size of the false finding from the two cuts. The ratio of the cuts and the ratio of remaining risks were both tried and neither works on its own.
6. 600 trials per case: each figure is uncertain by 1 to 2 points.
