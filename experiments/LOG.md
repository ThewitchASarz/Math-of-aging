## 2026-11-21: experiment 036, a grouped test of whether the benefit differs by frailty score

- **Question.** When a trial asks "does the benefit differ between sturdy and frail dogs?" by splitting a frailty score into five groups in both arms, is the test sound, and does it notice a difference as often as the straight-line test?
- **Predicted.** With no effect the five-groups-both-arms test fires in 3 to 8 percent of trials for all six scores, and the treated-only version in 11 to 20 percent with the rank score at quality 1.0. With risk cut 50 percent in every dog, the straight-line and five-groups-both-arms tests fire in 3 to 9 percent at score quality 0.7. With sturdy dogs helped more (cuts of 70 and 30 percent) the five-groups-both-arms test notices in 22 to 40 percent at rank quality 0.5, 62 to 80 at 0.7 and at least 97 at 1.0, and at 0.7 is 6 to 24 points below the straight-line test. With middle dogs helped more, the straight-line test fires in fewer than 12 percent at quality 0.7 and 1.0, and the five-groups-both-arms test notices in at least 95 percent at 1.0 and 20 to 45 at 0.7.
- **Happened.** All four confirmed. No effect: 4.7 to 6.2 percent for the both-arms test; 16.2 for treated only (and 11.3 at rank quality 0.9, 10.5 with the risk score at 0.7, which was not predicted). Equal cut: straight-line 5.5 and 6.2 percent, five groups 5.3 and 6.7. Sturdy helped more: 30.3, 71.7 and 100 percent, against 53.7, 87.5 and 100 for the straight-line test. Middle helped more: straight-line 11.0 and 8.5 percent (16.5 with the risk score at 0.7); five groups 26.3 percent at quality 0.7, 90.5 at 0.9 and 100 at 1.0. Three groups do a little better than five for a steady pattern (76.7 percent at quality 0.7) and worse for the middle pattern (79.8 at 0.9). With cuts of 60 and 40 percent at quality 0.7 the straight-line test notices in 35.7 percent and five groups in 19.5. No dog's rate of aging changed in any case.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code written and run before ours was opened, eleven cases, 1,500 to 2,500 trials each; 43 of 44 comparisons within 2 standard errors and one at 2.0; no errors found; inputs matched their file. With its larger runs it found the sound tests slightly above 5 percent when nothing differs (straight-line 6.3 to 7.3, five groups up to 6.6), so "about 5 percent" is stated as "about 5 to 6". It had "only the grouped test sees the middle pattern" narrowed to scores of high quality, and had a warning added that a test which does not fire does not show the benefit is equal. It did not re-run rank quality 0.9. Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 were not tried again in this run (experiments 022 and 023 still wait).
- **Changed.** A grouped test of "the benefit differs by score" must group the score in both arms; the treated-only version is faulty for several scores and should not be used. The straight-line test is the better main warning for a steady pattern (about 88 against 72 percent at quality 0.7); the grouped test is worth running beside it for patterns that are not steady, but needs a score of quality about 0.9 to see them. At quality 0.7 a 60 and 40 split is missed by both tests in most trials. Every spread, benefit size, pattern and score form is an assumption.

## 2026-11-20: experiment 035, a score-adjusted slope test with a setting for left-over hidden differences

- **Question.** When a slope test is adjusted for a frailty score and gives entry age its own setting, does adding a setting for the hidden differences the score leaves over make false reports of slowed aging rarer or more common?
- **Predicted.** With no effect the test with the spread setting flags 3 to 8 percent of trials with no score and with the rank score at quality 0.7 and 1.0. With sturdy dogs helped more (cuts of 70 and 30 percent) it reports slowed aging in 80 to 95 percent with no score, 48 to 70 at quality 0.7 and 24 to 42 at quality 0.9, more than the test without it. With risk cut 50 percent in every dog, the test without the spread setting reports faster aging in 12 to 28 percent with no score and 8 to 22 at quality 0.7, and the test with it flags 3 to 9. A true 20 percent slowing is recognised by the test with the spread setting in 55 to 74 percent at quality 0.7, no more than 8 points below the test without it, and in 58 to 80 with no score.
- **Happened.** All four confirmed. No effect: 5.2, 4.3 and 5.0 percent. Sturdy helped more, with the spread setting: 87.2, 59.3 and 27.5 percent, against 52.2, 39.5 and 21.7 without it; at quality 1.0 the two are the same (about 10 percent). Risk cut 50 percent, without the spread setting: 24.2 and 11.5 percent, all as faster aging; with it 5.0 and 5.3. True slowing: 67.5 percent with the spread setting against 62.2 without at quality 0.7, and 69.7 against 56.2 with no score. So the two versions err in opposite directions: without the spread setting a plain risk cut is read as faster aging, and with it an unequal benefit is read as slowed aging more often. No dog's rate of aging changed in any case but the true slowing.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code written and run before ours was opened, 21 figures, about 1,500 trials each; all within 2 standard errors; no errors found; inputs matched their file. It had the reading of the power gain changed: more power for a true slowing and more false reports under an unequal benefit are one property, not a gain and a cost. It put the spread test with a 50 percent cut at quality 0.7 at 7.1 percent against our 5.3 (about 6.6 pooled, tilted towards faster aging), so that figure is stated as about 5 to 7; and the no-score test without spread for a true slowing at 52.3 against 56.2. It confirmed that dropping experiment 012's before-entry term is sound when entry age has its own setting. Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 could not be opened in this run (experiments 022 and 023 still wait).
- **Changed.** A setting for left-over hidden differences is not a repair for the unequal-benefit trap in a score-adjusted slope test: at score quality 0.7 it takes false reports of slowed aging from about 40 to about 59 percent. It is still needed against the opposite mistake. Neither version is a safe default, and a finding of slowed aging in a trial where the benefit differs by score should not be relied on. Every spread, benefit size and score form is an assumption.

## 2026-11-20: experiment 038, does a larger trial make the trap worse

- **Question.** When a treatment helps sturdy and frail dogs unequally but changes no dog's rate of aging, does enrolling more dogs make a slope test report a changed rate of aging more often, and how does that compare with the gain in power for a true slowing?

At 40 and 60 it reports faster aging in 5 to 18, 28 to 50 and 48 to 72 percent; at 42 and 18 slowed aging in 9 to 24, 42 to 65 and 68 to 90. In 4-year trials at 60 and 40: fewer than 10 percent with 600 dogs, 4 to 14 with 1,300, 8 to 20 with 2,600 and 14 to 32 with 5,000, against 8 to 20, 36 to 58 and 62 to 84 percent for recognising a true 20 percent slowing with 600, 2,600 and 5,000. With an equal 50 percent cut and 5,000 dogs over 10 years the hidden-differences test flags 3 to 8 percent and the plain test reports faster aging in 48 to 75.
- **Happened.** All four confirmed. Ten years, 60 and 40: 15.2, 53.7 and 80.0 percent (28.7 at 1,300 from experiment 033); 55 and 45: 7.7, 17.7 and 27.0. 40 and 60: 11.0, 33.7 and 51.0; 42 and 18: 15.3, 50.0 and 81.7. Cases at 600 and 2,600 dogs were not re-run. Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 could not be opened in this run (experiments 022 and 023 still wait).

Figures at other sizes can be estimated roughly from the 1,300-dog ones by a square-root rule (within 4 points in 17 of 18 cases). A 4-year trial is less exposed only because it is noisier. Every spread, cut and design is an assumption.

## 2026-11-19: experiment 033, how large a difference in benefit breaks the slope test

- **Question.** How large must the difference in benefit between sturdy and frail dogs be before a slope test reports slowed aging that is not there in more than 10 percent of trials?
- **Predicted.** In 10-year trials with a spread of hidden risk of 0.3, the hidden-differences test reports slowed aging in 6 to 18 percent of trials with cuts of 55 and 45 percent and 20 to 45 with 60 and 40; the plain test in fewer than 12 percent at 60 and 40 and 15 to 35 at 65 and 35. At spread 0.1 with 70 and 30: plain 15 to 45 percent, hidden-differences 30 to 65; with 60 and 40 both below 25. A cut that falls steadily with rank is no safer than two halves (at least 85 percent for 90-to-10; 25 to 55 for 70-to-30). In a 4-year trial with 70 and 30: plain below 8 percent, hidden-differences 5 to 20; with sizes and dropout over 10 years: 35 to 60 and 65 to 90.
- **Happened.** Two confirmed, one half and half, one refuted. Spread 0.3: hidden-differences test 9.8 percent at 55 and 45, 28.7 at 60 and 40, 63.7 at 65 and 35; plain test 1.7, 3.3 and 24.5 (confirmed). Spread 0.1 with 70 and 30: plain 66.0 and hidden-differences 79.5 percent, both above the predicted range, so a narrow spread does not weaken the trap (refuted); with 60 and 40: 11.2 and 21.7 (confirmed). Steady patterns: 96.8 percent for 90-to-10, and 45.5 for 70-to-30 against 28.7 for two halves of 60 and 40 (confirmed; steady is worse). 4-year trial: 11.8 and 23.0 percent, higher than predicted; sizes and dropout: 30.5 and 62.3, lower than predicted (refuted). No dog's rate of aging changed in any case.

It showed that one reference figure of ours (the plain test flagging faster aging in 25.3 percent of trials with an equal 50 percent cut) was a high chance draw: about 21 percent over 4,600 trials. Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 could not be opened in this run (experiments 022 and 023 still wait).
- **Changed.** The trap of experiment 017 does not need a large difference in benefit or a wide spread of hidden risk: 60 against 40 percent misleads the hidden-differences test in about 3 trials in 10, and the treatment itself creates the differences between dogs that cause it. The plain test's apparent sturdiness at 60 and 40 is two errors cancelling. A 4-year trial is about as likely to report slowed aging from a 70-against-30 difference as from a true 20 percent slowing. A slope finding from death records alone needs outside support. Every spread, cut and pattern is an assumption.

## 2026-11-19: experiment 034, a score-adjusted slope test with entry age given its own setting

- **Predicted.** With no effect and a rank score of quality 0.9 or 1.0, the entry-age test flags 3 to 8 percent of trials with a median estimated rate of 0.98 to 1.03. ## 2026-11-19: experiment 032, a frailty score against a benefit that differs between dogs

- **Question.** If each dog has a frailty score measured at entry that tracks its hidden risk imperfectly, how good must the score be before adding "benefit differs by score" to the slope test stops a treatment that helps sturdy dogs more from being read as slowed aging?
- **Predicted.** With sturdy dogs helped more (cuts of 70 and 30 percent) and no score, the plain test flags slowed aging in 50 to 65 percent of 10-year trials and the hidden-differences test in 82 to 93. With score terms in the plain test: more than 30 percent at score quality 0.5, 20 to 45 at 0.7, 8 to 25 at 0.9, 3 to 10 at 1.0. At quality 0.7 the score terms give 3 to 8 percent false alarms with no effect and cost no more than 5 points of power for a true 20 percent slowing.
- **Happened.** No score: 56.2 and 87.8 percent (confirmed). With score terms: 46.5 percent at quality 0.5 and 32.2 at 0.7 (confirmed); 8.0 at 0.9 and 1.8 at 1.0 (half and half: a perfect score leaves fewer false reports than predicted). No effect: 4.7 and 5.5 percent; power 70.5 against 72.0 for the plain test and 75.8 against 82.3 for the hidden-differences test (half and half: the second loses more than 5 points). Not predicted: with a very good score and no effect, the test with score terms flags 10 percent of trials, nearly all as faster aging. The trial notices that the benefit differs by score in 50 percent of trials at quality 0.5 and 88 percent at 0.7. With hidden risk itself as the score, the lean disappears and 15 percent of trials still report slowed aging.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code, every case, 300 trials each; no errors found; inputs matched their file. The check put quality 0.9 at 12.0 percent, so that figure is stated as about 8 to 12; it found power costs of about 7 and 10 points, so those are stated as ranges. It confirmed the cause of the lean in one trial of 52,000 dogs: the score ranks dogs within their entry age, so risk rises with entry age more slowly than with time in the trial; giving entry age its own setting moved the estimated rate from 1.066 to 1.008. Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 could not be opened in this run (experiments 022 and 023 still wait).
- **Changed.** A frailty score protects a slope test only at quality 0.9 or better. A moderately good score works as a warning (it shows that the benefit differs), not as a repair. An analysis that adjusts for a score should let risk depend on entry age separately from time in the trial. Every spread, benefit size and score quality is an assumption; nobody has measured the quality of a real frailty score for dogs.

## 2026-11-18: experiment 013, breeds that age at different rates

- **Question.** If dog breeds differ in how fast their death risk rises with age, and not only in their level of risk, does the slope test misread a plain cut in risk as a change in the rate of aging, and does giving each common breed its own level and rate repair it?
- **Predicted.** With no true effect all three analyses stay at 3 to 8 percent. With the whole breed difference put on the rate, ignoring breed misreads a 50 percent risk cut in 8 to 20 percent of 1,300-dog, 10-year trials, and a level per breed does not repair it (over 8 percent). A long trial should plan a level per breed with one shared rate, and should not give every breed its own rate. The share of the breed difference that sits on the rate is an assumption; a trial that enrols mostly large or short-lived dogs is untested.

## 2026-11-18: experiment 009, a benefit that fades slowly or in part

- **Question.** In a trial that doses for one year and then watches, what does the two-period test report when the benefit fades slowly or only part of the way, and does a longer or a larger trial read it better?
- **Predicted.** A benefit that half lasts is recognised as lasting in 25 to 45 percent of 580-dog, 3-year trials, over 85 percent with 10 years and over 95 with 5,000 dogs. A lasting tenth is missed everywhere (under 15, and under 50 percent). A slow fade (halving every 2 years) is reported as lasting in 45 to 70 percent of 3-year trials with a ratio of 0.60 to 0.72, and still in 35 to 70 percent of 10-year trials (ratio 0.78 to 0.92). A quick fade (halving every 6 months) is reported as lasting in under 20 percent of 580-dog trials but 50 to 85 percent of 5,000-dog trials.
- **Happened.** Half lasts: 38, 93 and 99 percent (confirmed). A lasting tenth: 9, 25 and 46 percent (confirmed). Slow fade: 65 percent with ratio 0.65; 59 percent with ratio 0.83 over 10 years (confirmed). Quick fade: 18.5 percent with 580 dogs (confirmed) and 87 percent with 5,000 (on the line, just above the predicted range; half and half). Not predicted: 10 years of watching lets a quick fade wash out (7 percent), and in the published design a quick fade and a small permanent benefit look the same (ratios 0.83 and 0.85).
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code, about 40 numbers, 1,500 to 4,000 trials per case; no errors; one decision in about 3,600 shared trials differed. The check put the 5,000-dog quick-fade figure at about 86 percent (on the line), showed one of our figures (24.8 percent) sits about 2 points above its own (chance, traced), and showed the after-dosing ratio is close to an average of the true effect weighted by when dogs die. Its own quick look at splitting the after-dosing time in two: a quick fade is flagged in 66 percent of 5,000-dog trials and 14 percent of 580-dog trials. Neither we nor the check could open the trial's design paper in this run.
- **Changed.** "Lasting" from the two-period test means "still there on average while we watched", not "permanent". The ratio it gives depends on how long the trial watches. More years help (a real partial benefit is recognised, a quick fade washes out); more dogs alone make the average precise without telling fading from lasting. A trial should say this in advance and plan a look at drift over time. All effect sizes and fading speeds are assumptions.

## 2026-11-17: experiment 014, dropout linked to health or to treatment

- **Question.** In a 1,300-dog, 10-year trial, when dogs leave for reasons linked to their health or to the treatment, do the standard analyses report slowed aging or a cut in risk that is not there?
- **Predicted.** Dropout linked only to treatment, or to health equally in both groups, leaves the slope test at 3 to 8 percent false alarms. Sick dogs withdrawn before death more often in the treated group (30 against 15 percent) make a useless treatment look like a risk cut in over half of trials (risk ratio 0.78 to 0.88) but leave the slope test alone. The slope test is fooled only when the share of missed deaths drifts over time in one group: 6 to 15 percent for a drift to 15 percent, 15 to 40 for a drift to 30. A true 20 percent slowing is still recognised in 65 to 82 percent.
- **Happened.** Slope test 5 to 7 percent in the fair-looking cases. Unequal withdrawal: a false risk cut in 69 percent of trials, middle risk ratio 0.83, slope test 6 percent. Drift: 11 and 29 percent, always pointing to slowed aging. Frail treated dogs leaving: 6 percent. True slowing recognised in 68 to 75 percent (82 with fair dropout). All four confirmed.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code, 25 numbers, 600 to 3,000 trials per case; no errors. The check showed two of our first figures (8.3 and 8.9 percent false alarms, from 800 trials) were noise: larger runs by both sides give about 6. It also showed the slope test fires in 5 to 7 percent even with fair dropout. No outside source was opened in this run.
- **Changed.** The danger from unfair dropout is mostly to the ordinary "did it cut deaths" result, not to the slope test. A trial should record what happens to every dog that leaves, in both groups, and compare withdrawals between the groups year by year. Every dropout pattern here is an assumption; no published pattern for a dog trial was found.

## 2026-11-17: experiment 019, risk that rebounds after stopping

- **Question.** In a trial that doses for one year and watches for three, what do the standard analyses report when risk rebounds after dosing stops, or when only part of the benefit lasts, and does the mix of surviving dogs bias the after-dosing comparison?
- **Predicted.** With risk halved in the dosing year and then 1.5 times the untreated level, the main test fires in 20 to 45 percent of trials and nearly always points to harm. The two-period test sees that lasting rebound in 60 to 85 percent and a short one (double for six months) in under 35. When half of a halving lasts, the main test fires in 50 to 75 percent and the benefit is recognised as lasting in 20 to 45. Survivor mix does not matter: false alarms under 8 percent and a survivor risk ratio under 1.03.
- **Happened.** Main test 28 percent, every time pointing to harm. Two-period test 76 percent for the lasting rebound, 30 for the short one. Half-lasting benefit: 63 and 35 percent. Survivor mix: 5.8 percent false alarms and a ratio of 1.013 to 1.015. All four confirmed. Not predicted: under a short rebound the main test sits at chance (5.5 percent), because the benefit and the rebound cancel.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code and 8,000 trials per case, about 30 numbers; no errors. The check showed our survivor ratio and our brute-force test of it measured two different things (same-age dogs against whole groups); both are now computed and reported. Neither we nor the check could open the trial's design paper or the three animal papers in this run.
- **Changed.** A trial that stops dosing should plan the two-period comparison in advance: the test over the whole trial can cancel a benefit against a rebound or read a helpful-while-given treatment as harmful. Survivor mix is cleared as a practical threat for this design. New limits: one after-dosing number cannot tell a short large rebound from a lasting small one, and "no lasting benefit found" is weak evidence at this size. All effect sizes are assumptions; no rebound is known in dogs.

## 2026-11-16: experiment 012, a slope test that allows for hidden differences

- **Question.** If the slope test is given an extra setting for hidden differences between dogs, does it stop misreading a plain cut in risk as a change in the rate of aging, and how much power does it keep?
- **Predicted.** With widely spread hidden differences (0.3) the plain test gives over 15 percent false alarms and the new test 3 to 8; the new test stays at 3 to 8 percent wherever its assumed shape is right; the repair costs at least 5 points of power when it is not needed; with a different shape the plain test is above 15 percent and the new test below 10.
- **Happened.** Plain 19 percent, new 5.5 (confirmed). New test 3.5 to 6.9 percent in all six cases (confirmed). No cost in power: 88.5 against 88.0 percent with no hidden differences, and 81 to 85 percent with them (refuted in its main part, in the useful direction). With a different shape the plain test gives 12.5 percent, not over 15, and the new test 4.5 (half and half).
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code in six cases; no errors; inputs confirmed. The check stopped us calling the higher power in the wide-spread worlds a clean gain, since the plain test is the wrong model there, and showed false alarms can only be stated as "about 5 percent".
- **Changed.** The threat left open by experiments 004 and 008 has a repair for a long trial: add one setting for hidden differences. The plain test's false alarms pointed to faster aging, not slower. Untested: short trials, dropout and sizes with the new test, and treatments that help frail dogs more.

## 2026-11-11: experiment 008, differences between breeds
