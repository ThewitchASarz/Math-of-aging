## 2026-11-19: experiment 032, a frailty score against a benefit that differs between dogs

- **Question.** If each dog has a frailty score measured at entry that tracks its hidden risk imperfectly, how good must the score be before adding "benefit differs by score" to the slope test stops a treatment that helps sturdy dogs more from being read as slowed aging?
- **Predicted.** With sturdy dogs helped more (cuts of 70 and 30 percent) and no score, the plain test flags slowed aging in 50 to 65 percent of 10-year trials and the hidden-differences test in 82 to 93. With score terms in the plain test: more than 30 percent at score quality 0.5, 20 to 45 at 0.7, 8 to 25 at 0.9, 3 to 10 at 1.0. At quality 0.7 the score terms give 3 to 8 percent false alarms with no effect and cost no more than 5 points of power for a true 20 percent slowing.
- **Happened.** No score: 56.2 and 87.8 percent (confirmed). With score terms: 46.5 percent at quality 0.5 and 32.2 at 0.7 (confirmed); 8.0 at 0.9 and 1.8 at 1.0 (half and half: a perfect score leaves fewer false reports than predicted). No effect: 4.7 and 5.5 percent; power 70.5 against 72.0 for the plain test and 75.8 against 82.3 for the hidden-differences test (half and half: the second loses more than 5 points). Not predicted: with a very good score and no effect, the test with score terms flags 10 percent of trials, nearly all as faster aging. The trial notices that the benefit differs by score in 50 percent of trials at quality 0.5 and 88 percent at 0.7. With hidden risk itself as the score, the lean disappears and 15 percent of trials still report slowed aging.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code, every case, 300 trials each; no errors found; inputs matched their file. The check put quality 0.9 at 12.0 percent, so that figure is stated as about 8 to 12; it found power costs of about 7 and 10 points, so those are stated as ranges. It confirmed the cause of the lean in one trial of 52,000 dogs: the score ranks dogs within their entry age, so risk rises with entry age more slowly than with time in the trial; giving entry age its own setting moved the estimated rate from 1.066 to 1.008. Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 could not be opened in this run (experiments 022 and 023 still wait).
- **Changed.** A frailty score protects a slope test only at quality 0.9 or better. A moderately good score works as a warning (it shows that the benefit differs), not as a repair. An analysis that adjusts for a score should let risk depend on entry age separately from time in the trial. Every spread, benefit size and score quality is an assumption; nobody has measured the quality of a real frailty score for dogs.

## 2026-11-18: experiment 017, the hidden-differences test in harder conditions

- **Question.** Does the slope test with a setting for hidden differences between dogs (experiment 012) still work in a 4-year trial, in a 10-year trial with dropout and a mix of sizes, and when the treatment helps frail dogs more than sturdy ones?
- **Predicted.** With widely spread hidden risk (0.3) and a 50 percent risk cut, a 4-year trial: plain test flags 6.5 to 15 percent, new test 3 to 8. A 20 percent slowing is recognised by the new test in 15 to 32 percent of 4-year trials, with no loss against the plain test. Ten years with five sizes and 5 percent dropout a year: plain 12 to 25 percent, new 3 to 8, and 60 to 80 percent power. Frail dogs helped more (cuts of 70 and 30 percent, no change in anyone's rate of aging): plain 25 to 60 percent, new above 10, and under 3 percent flagged as slowed aging.
- **Happened.** Four years: plain 9.1, new 4.1 percent; power 25.8 percent, and 29.4 against 28.4 with no hidden differences (both confirmed). Sizes and dropout: plain 8.7 percent (below the predicted range), new 3.6, power 80.5 (half and half). Frail helped more: plain 77.6 percent (above the range), new 30.5, none as slowed aging (half and half). Not predicted: when sturdy dogs are helped more, the plain test reports slowed aging in 58 percent of 10-year trials and the new test in 88 percent, with no dog aging more slowly.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code, all ten cases, 400 to 800 trials each; formula re-derived; no errors that change a number; inputs matched their files. The check put the plain test in the sizes-and-dropout case at 12.3 percent, so that figure is stated as about 9 to 12. It reproduced the sturdy-helped-more result (55.7 and 88.0 percent) and showed without chance that the risk ratio between the groups drifts from 0.58 to 0.43 over 8 years in that case, and from 0.42 to 0.73 when frail dogs are helped more. One wrong code comment corrected. Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 could not be opened in this run.
- **Changed.** The repair of experiment 012 can be used in short trials and with dropout and sizes. But both slope tests assume the treatment helps every dog by the same fraction. A treatment that helps sturdy dogs more is read as slowed aging in most 10-year trials. A finding of slowed aging from a slope test therefore needs a check of whether the benefit differs between frail and sturdy dogs. Every spread and every size of benefit is an assumption.

## 2026-11-18: experiment 015, a delayed effect and owners who do not treat

- **Question.** In the 10-year trial of experiment 011, what happens to the slope test when the treatment takes a year or two to reach full strength, or when some owners do not give it?
- **Predicted.** A build-up of 1 or 2 years costs a true 20 percent slowing little (72 to 82 and 66 to 80 percent recognised, against 79 with no delay). Owners cost a lot: 64 to 76 percent with 10 percent of treated dogs never given the treatment, 52 to 66 at 20 percent, 40 to 56 at 30 percent, 45 to 65 with 10 percent of owners stopping each year; with a 1-year build-up and 20 percent never given, 50 to 65 percent at 1,300 dogs and 2,000 or 2,600 dogs needed. A 50 percent risk cut building up over 2 years is misread as slowed aging in 20 to 50 percent of trials (8 to 25 over 1 year). Leaving out the first 2 years brings that to 3 to 8 percent and leaves 45 to 68 percent power for an immediate slowing.
- **Happened.** Build-up: 78 and 79 percent (confirmed; no cost found). Never given: 74, 67 and 61 percent; stopping: 46 percent; combined: 72 percent at 1,300 dogs, 76 at 1,600, 84 at 2,000 (half and half: owners who never treat cost less than predicted, about 6 points per 10 percent; the stopping figure and the trial size were as predicted). Risk cut building over 2 years: flagged as slowed aging in 23 percent of trials, over 1 year in 8.6 (confirmed). From year 2: 5.5 percent, with 51 percent power (confirmed).
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code, every case, 800 to 3,000 trials; no errors; inputs matched exactly. The check showed with 3,000 trials that our run's hint of a build-up helping (72 against 67 percent) was noise: 68.6 against 68.8. Trial size for the combined case is stated as 1,600 to 2,000 dogs. Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 could not be opened in this run.
- **Changed.** A slow start is no threat to detecting a true slowing, but a risk cut with a slow start can be labelled slowed aging in about one trial in four. A trial planning a slope test should plan a second analysis that leaves out the build-up period, and should know it costs about a third of the power. Owners who stop along the way are the largest loss found so far in the 10-year design. Every delay and share is an assumption.

## 2026-11-18: experiment 013, breeds that age at different rates

- **Question.** If dog breeds differ in how fast their death risk rises with age, and not only in their level of risk, does the slope test misread a plain cut in risk as a change in the rate of aging, and does giving each common breed its own level and rate repair it?
- **Predicted.** With no true effect all three analyses stay at 3 to 8 percent. With the whole breed difference put on the rate, ignoring breed misreads a 50 percent risk cut in 8 to 20 percent of 1,300-dog, 10-year trials, and a level per breed does not repair it (over 8 percent). A level and a rate per breed brings it to 3 to 8 percent and recognises a true 20 percent slowing in 75 to 90 percent of 10-year trials and 15 to 35 percent of 4-year trials.
- **Happened.** No effect: 4 to 7 percent (confirmed). Ignoring breed: 7.2 percent, not 8 to 20, and a level per breed gives 6.1 (refuted: the disturbance is smaller than predicted and the simple analysis is enough). A level and a rate per breed: 6.6 to 7.6 percent (confirmed by the letter). Power 81.5 and 26 percent (confirmed). Not predicted: a level per breed with one shared rate has the best power (85 to 89 percent), and ignoring breed costs about 9 points.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code, 3,000 trials per cell and about 40 numbers; no errors; breed settings matched to 9 digits; the check reported opening the breed table's source and matching all 19 rows. The check showed the level-and-rate analysis gives about 6.5 to 7 percent false alarms on its own account, even with no breed differences, so it is not a clean repair. It also measured the bias with noise removed (rate multiplier 1.025 where the truth is 1.00, a third of the chance scatter). Raz et al. 2026, McMillan et al. 2024 and the rapamycin trial's design paper could not be opened in this run.
- **Changed.** Breed differences in the rate of aging, as far as published breed life expectancies allow, are not a practical threat to the slope test: the fast-aging breeds are rare among dogs old enough to enter. A long trial should plan a level per breed with one shared rate, and should not give every breed its own rate. The share of the breed difference that sits on the rate is an assumption; a trial that enrols mostly large or short-lived dogs is untested.

## 2026-11-18: experiment 009, a benefit that fades slowly or in part

- **Question.** In a trial that doses for one year and then watches, what does the two-period test report when the benefit fades slowly or only part of the way, and does a longer or a larger trial read it better?
- **Predicted.** A benefit that half lasts is recognised as lasting in 25 to 45 percent of 580-dog, 3-year trials, over 85 percent with 10 years and over 95 with 5,000 dogs. A lasting tenth is missed everywhere (under 15, and under 50 percent). A slow fade (halving every 2 years) is reported as lasting in 45 to 70 percent of 3-year trials with a ratio of 0.60 to 0.72, and still in 35 to 70 percent of 10-year trials (ratio 0.78 to 0.92). A quick fade (halving every 6 months) is reported as lasting in under 20 percent of 580-dog trials but 50 to 85 percent of 5,000-dog trials.
- **Happened.** Half lasts: 38, 93 and 99 percent (confirmed). A lasting tenth: 9, 25 and 46 percent (confirmed). Slow fade: 65 percent with ratio 0.65; 59 percent with ratio 0.83 over 10 years (confirmed). Quick fade: 18.5 percent with 580 dogs (confirmed) and 87 percent with 5,000 (on the line, just above the predicted range; half and half). Not predicted: 10 years of watching lets a quick fade wash out (7 percent), and in the published design a quick fade and a small permanent benefit look the same (ratios 0.83 and 0.85).
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code, about 40 numbers, 1,500 to 4,000 trials per case; no errors; one decision in about 3,600 shared trials differed. The check put the 5,000-dog quick-fade figure at about 86 percent (on the line), showed one of our figures (24.8 percent) sits about 2 points above its own (chance, traced), and showed the after-dosing ratio is close to an average of the true effect weighted by when dogs die. Its own quick look at splitting the after-dosing time in two: a quick fade is flagged in 66 percent of 5,000-dog trials and 14 percent of 580-dog trials. Neither we nor the check could open the trial's design paper in this run.
- **Changed.** "Lasting" from the two-period test means "still there on average while we watched", not "permanent". The ratio it gives depends on how long the trial watches. More years help (a real partial benefit is recognised, a quick fade washes out); more dogs alone make the average precise without telling fading from lasting. A trial should say this in advance and plan a look at drift over time. All effect sizes and fading speeds are assumptions.

## 2026-11-17: experiment 014, dropout linked to health or to treatment

- **Question.** In a 1,300-dog, 10-year trial, when dogs leave for reasons linked to their health or to the treatment, do the standard analyses report slowed aging or a cut in risk that is not there?
- **Predicted.** Dropout linked only to treatment, or to health equally in both groups, leaves the slope test at 3 to 8 percent false alarms. Sick dogs withdrawn before death more often in the treated group (30 against 15 percent) make a useless treatment look like a risk cut in over half of trials (risk ratio 0.78 to 0.88) but leave the slope test alone. The slope test is fooled only when the share of missed deaths drifts over time in one group: 6 to 15 percent for a drift to 15 percent, 15 to 40 for a drift to 30. A true 20 percent slowing is still recognised in 65 to 82 percent.
- **Happened.** Slope test 5 to 7 percent in the fair-looking cases. Unequal withdrawal: a false risk cut in 69 percent of trials, middle risk ratio 0.83, slope test 6 percent. Drift: 11 and 29 percent, always pointing to slowed aging. Frail treated dogs leaving: 6 percent. True slowing recognised in 68 to 75 percent (82 with fair dropout). All four confirmed.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code, 25 numbers, 600 to 3,000 trials per case; no errors. The check showed two of our first figures (8.3 and 8.9 percent false alarms, from 800 trials) were noise: larger runs by both sides give about 6. It also showed the slope test fires in 5 to 7 percent even with fair dropout. No outside source was opened in this run.
- **Changed.** The danger from unfair dropout is mostly to the ordinary "did it cut deaths" result, not to the slope test. A trial should record what happens to every dog that leaves, in both groups, and compare withdrawals between the groups year by year. Every dropout pattern here is an assumption; no published pattern for a dog trial was found.

## 2026-11-17: experiment 019, risk that rebounds after stopping

- **Question.** In a trial that doses for one year and watches for three, what do the standard analyses report when risk rebounds after dosing stops, or when only part of the benefit lasts, and does the mix of surviving dogs bias the after-dosing comparison?
- **Predicted.** With risk halved in the dosing year and then 1.5 times the untreated level, the main test fires in 20 to 45 percent of trials and nearly always points to harm. The two-period test sees that lasting rebound in 60 to 85 percent and a short one (double for six months) in under 35. When half of a halving lasts, the main test fires in 50 to 75 percent and the benefit is recognised as lasting in 20 to 45. Survivor mix does not matter: false alarms under 8 percent and a survivor risk ratio under 1.03.
- **Happened.** Main test 28 percent, every time pointing to harm. Two-period test 76 percent for the lasting rebound, 30 for the short one. Half-lasting benefit: 63 and 35 percent. Survivor mix: 5.8 percent false alarms and a ratio of 1.013 to 1.015. All four confirmed. Not predicted: under a short rebound the main test sits at chance (5.5 percent), because the benefit and the rebound cancel.
- **Check.** Reproduced independently with separate code and 8,000 trials per case, about 30 numbers; no errors. The check showed our survivor ratio and our brute-force test of it measured two different things (same-age dogs against whole groups); both are now computed and reported. Neither we nor the check could open the trial's design paper or the three animal papers in this run.
- **Changed.** A trial that stops dosing should plan the two-period comparison in advance: the test over the whole trial can cancel a benefit against a rebound or read a helpful-while-given treatment as harmful. Survivor mix is cleared as a practical threat for this design. New limits: one after-dosing number cannot tell a short large rebound from a lasting small one, and "no lasting benefit found" is weak evidence at this size. All effect sizes are assumptions; no rebound is known in dogs.

Give entry age its own setting and a "benefit differs by score" term that is not a straight line, and measure false alarms, false reports of slowed aging with sturdy dogs helped more, and power, at score qualities 0.5 to 1.0 and with a score that tracks hidden risk itself with error.
4. **033: how large a difference in benefit breaks the slope test.** Experiment 032 found a frailty score repairs this only at quality 0.9 or better, so the size of difference that matters is the next thing to know. Experiment 017 used cuts of 70 and 30 percent and a spread of 0.3. Repeat with smaller differences (60 and 40, 55 and 45), spreads of 0.1 and 0.3, a benefit that varies smoothly with hidden risk, and the 4-year and sizes-and-dropout designs with sturdy dogs helped more, to find the smallest difference that lifts false reports of slowed aging above 10 percent.
5. **018: how the settling trial's size depends on the inputs.** Experiment 016 found 1,300 dogs reach 78 percent with the rapamycin trial's published inputs against 88 with ours. Vary the slope (0.29 to 0.36) and the entry ages separately to find which one matters and how many dogs each case needs.
6. **020: how many dogs a stop-and-watch trial needs.** Experiment 019 found a 580-dog trial sees a 1.25-fold rebound in about a quarter of trials and a half-lasting benefit in about a third. Find the number of dogs and years of watching that bring each to 80 percent, with the rapamycin trial's published inputs.
7. **021: splitting the time after dosing.** Give the two-period test a third period (the first six months after stopping) and measure whether it can tell a short large rebound from a lasting small one, and what that costs in false alarms.
8. **024: how much tracing of withdrawn dogs is enough.** Experiment 014 found that sick dogs withdrawn unequally create a false risk cut, and a drifting share creates false slowed aging. Repeat with the trial finding the date of death for half, or nine in ten, of the withdrawn dogs, and measure how much of each false result remains. Also test whether a year-by-year comparison of withdrawals between the groups would flag the problem.
9. **025: the cell model with deaths unrelated to aging.** Experiment 005 found the directly solved model misses dog death rates mostly at ages 2 to 4, in the same way in every fit. Add a constant background death rate as a fitted setting, solve directly, and measure whether the miss disappears, whether the fitted sizes (clearance about half, lethal load about 40 percent lower, swings about 1.8 times) move, and whether any of the three explanations then stands out.
10. **026: does the after-dosing benefit drift?** Experiment 009 found the two-period test cannot tell a fading benefit from a permanent one, and its check found that separate ratios for the first and second year after dosing flag a quick fade in 66 percent of 5,000-dog trials but 14 percent of 580-dog trials. Test a trend in the after-dosing ratio properly: false alarms, and the dogs and years needed to tell a fade from a permanent benefit of the same average size. Could be run together with 021.
11. **027: partial and slow fades in harder conditions.** Repeat experiment 009 with 5 to 10 percent dropout a year, hidden differences between dogs, risk that bends at old ages (for the 10-year design) and the 210 dogs enrolled at the last published count.
12. **028: the slope test in a trial of large dogs only.** Experiment 013 found breed differences in the rate of aging do little harm in a trial that enrols all breeds. The rapamycin trial enrols dogs of 20 to 50 kg only. Repeat with a trial restricted to large or short-lived breeds, and with a breed's rate higher and its level lower than its life expectancy alone would suggest, and measure false alarms and power with a level per breed.
13. **029: how many breeds can have their own rate.** Experiment 013's check found that giving about 12 groups their own rate lifts false alarms to about 6.5 to 7 percent with 1,300 dogs. Measure how that grows with the number of groups and shrinks with the number of dogs, and whether a small-sample correction brings it back to 5 percent.
14. **030: owners who stop, in more detail.** Experiment 015 found 10 percent of owners stopping each year takes the 10-year slope test from about 80 to 46 percent. Repeat with stopping rates of 2 to 10 percent a year, with stopping that is more likely when the dog is ill, and with an analysis that uses the recorded stopping date, and find the trial size each case needs.
15. **031: a cut-off for the build-up that is wrong.** Experiment 015 repaired the misreading of a slowly building risk cut by leaving out the first 2 years, with the build-up time known. Test a cut-off shorter or longer than the true build-up, a build-up that is not a straight line, wider hidden differences, and how many dogs the from-year-2 analysis needs for 80 percent.
