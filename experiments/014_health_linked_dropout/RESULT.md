# Experiment 014: result

**Unfair dropout fools the ordinary "did it cut deaths" result far more easily than it fools the slope test. If sick dogs are withdrawn before death in 30 percent of cases in the treated group and 15 percent in the untreated group, a treatment that does nothing is reported as cutting risk in about 69 percent of simulated 1,300-dog, 10-year trials (middle estimate: a 17 percent cut). The slope test is not fooled by that: it fires in about 6 percent, as it does with fair dropout. The slope test is fooled only when the share of missed deaths changes over time in one group: a drift from 0 to 30 percent over 10 years in the treated group makes it report slowed aging in about 29 percent of trials.** All four predictions were confirmed. Two first figures sat on or over the edge of their bands and turned out to be noise when more trials were run.

This is a calculation under stated assumptions. Every dropout pattern here is an assumption. No published dropout pattern for a dog trial was found, so nothing here says what happens in any real trial, and nothing here says that any treatment works.

## What this means

1. **Dropout linked to treatment alone is harmless to both tests.** If treated dogs leave twice as often but for reasons unrelated to their health, both tests keep their 5 percent false-alarm rate. The trial only loses some power (82 to 75 percent for a 20 percent slowing).
2. **Sick dogs leaving before death is harmless only when it is equal in both groups.** Then it removes the same share of deaths on each side. The cost is power: with 30 percent of deaths missing, recognition of a true slowing falls from 82 to 69 percent.
3. **Unequal withdrawal of sick dogs creates a false result in the level of risk.** A 30-against-15 percent gap makes a useless treatment look like a 17 percent cut in risk in about 7 trials in 10. The mirror image makes it look harmful just as often. It also makes a true halving look like a 57 percent cut. The reason is simple arithmetic: if 30 percent of deaths go unrecorded on one side and 15 percent on the other, the recorded death rates stand in the ratio 0.70 to 0.85, which is 0.82.
4. **The slope test is hard to fool this way.** Losing a fixed share of deaths shifts the level of recorded risk, not the rate at which it rises. So the slope test stays near its usual rate in every pattern where the missing share is steady.
5. **The slope test is fooled when the missing share changes with time in one group.** If more and more treated dogs' deaths go unrecorded as the years pass, recorded risk in the treated group rises more slowly than true risk. That is exactly what slowed aging looks like. A drift to 15 percent gives about 11 percent false reports of slowed aging; a drift to 30 percent gives about 29 percent.
6. **Frail treated dogs leaving more does little** with hidden differences of the size fitted in experiment 004 (6 percent, against 5 to 7 for the reference).

For a real trial this points to one practical step: find out what happened to every dog that leaves, in both groups, and record the date of death even after withdrawal. A trial that does this is protected against every pattern above. It also suggests a simple warning sign to look at: the number of withdrawals in each group, year by year. A gap between the groups, or a gap that grows, is the condition under which these false results appear.

## How this compares with published work

`papers/literature-check.md` names no published work on dropout in dog longevity trials, and no dropout rate for one was found. That censoring linked to health can bias a survival analysis is long-established textbook statistics ("informative censoring"); this experiment only puts numbers on it for one dog-trial design. The literature check's wider finding, that no published figures were found for how often a trial would recognise a slower rise of risk, applies here too: these figures were not found in the searches run, and those searches were limited.

## Independent check

A separate agent wrote its own simulation and its own likelihood and fitting code from a description, before reading ours. It ran 600 to 3,000 trials per case. Of 25 numbers compared, 24 agreed with ours within two standard errors. It read our code afterwards and found no errors in the simulation of each dropout pattern, the bookkeeping, the hidden-factor formula, the likelihood or the tests. It confirmed the size shares, the size-class rates and the hidden-factor spread against this repository's files.

What the check changed:

- **Two edge figures were withdrawn as noise.** Our 800-trial run gave 8.3 percent false alarms for "sick dogs leave, equally" and 8.9 percent for "frail treated dogs leave". The check drew 6.2 and 5.6 percent with 3,000 trials each, and our own second run of 2,500 trials (`borderline.py`) gave 6.5 and 6.2. We now quote the larger runs. The three cases (reference, sick equally, frail) cannot be told apart.
- **The reference rate is stated as "5 to 7 percent", not 5.** The slope test fires a little more often than 5 percent even with fair dropout, probably because it does not model the hidden differences between dogs. So a rate of 6 or 7 percent in a dropout case is not evidence of harm from dropout.
- It confirmed the arithmetic behind the false risk cut (0.70 / 0.85 = 0.82, against 0.83 simulated) and explained the small gap: background dropout removes some dogs before they could be withdrawn.
- It added the plain statement of which inputs have no source (listed below).

**Not checked:** neither we nor the check opened any outside source in this run. The size table's header records that its paper (Montoya et al. 2023) was opened on 10 November 2026. The check did not run the 50 percent risk-cut cases other than "sick treated dogs leave more", nor the slowing case for "treated leave more" and "sick treated more".

## Limits

1. **Every dropout pattern is an ASSUMPTION**: the background 5 percent a year, the 10 percent rate, the 30 and 15 percent shares of sick dogs withdrawn, the 6-month window, the frail-dog rate and both drifts. They were chosen to see which kinds of dropout matter, not because any trial shows them.
2. **Why the two groups would differ is not modelled.** In a trial where owners do not know which group their dog is in, a gap would have to come from something owners can see, such as side effects. We did not model that; we only imposed the gap.
3. **The drift is a straight line and only in the treated group.** Other shapes were not tried.
4. **ASSUMPTIONS carried over from experiment 011**: entry ages 7 to 12, the size mix of the US dog population, size-class rates that are a calibration and not fits to full life tables, a hidden risk factor of the tentative size 0.102 inside every size class, treatment given throughout with perfect adherence.
5. **The analysis has the right shape for the simulated dogs** apart from the hidden factor and the dropout. A real analysis will fit less well.
6. **Only one trial size and length was run** (1,300 dogs, 10 years). In a short trial fewer deaths are seen, and the sizes of these effects will differ.
7. A wider hidden spread (0.3, as in experiment 012) was not combined with "frail treated dogs leave". With a wider spread that pattern could matter more.
