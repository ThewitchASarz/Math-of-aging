# Experiment 010: prediction

Written before any code for this experiment was run. (Experiments 005, 008 and 009 remain in the queue; this one was brought forward because it turns the earlier results into a design.)

## Question

How large and how long must a dog trial be to recognise a treatment that slows aging, as distinct from one that only cuts risk?

## Method, fixed in advance

- Simulation and slope test of experiment 001: treatment given continuously from enrolment, dogs enrolled at ages 7 to 12, mortality from the UK fit, baseline re-estimated in every trial.
- Grid: 300, 600, 1,000, 1,300, 2,000 and 5,000 dogs; 4, 6, 8 and 10 years of follow-up.
- True effects: aging slowed by 20 percent and by 30 percent.
- Outcome: the share of 300 simulated trials in which the slope test recognises the slowing. "Enough" means 80 percent.

## Predictions

1. **P1.** With 10 years of follow-up, the smallest trial on the grid that recognises a 20 percent slowing 80 percent of the time has 1,000 dogs.
2. **P2.** With 6 years of follow-up, 1,300 dogs recognise a 20 percent slowing in 55 to 80 percent of trials.
3. **P3.** For a 30 percent slowing, 600 dogs followed for 10 years are enough.
4. **P4.** With 4 years of follow-up, no trial on the grid is enough for a 20 percent slowing, not even 5,000 dogs.

## What would change our mind

If P4 is refuted, a short trial can be rescued by size alone, which would contradict the "length beats size" finding of experiment 001.
