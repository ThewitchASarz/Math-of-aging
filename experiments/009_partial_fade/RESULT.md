# Experiment 009: result

**When the two-period test says a benefit "lasts", it means only that the benefit was still there, on average, during the time the trial watched. It cannot say whether the benefit will stay. A benefit that is slowly fading (halving every 2 years) is reported as lasting in about 65 percent of 580-dog, 3-year trials, more often than a benefit that half lasts for good (38 percent). Watching for 10 years in place of 3 rescues a benefit that half lasts (38 percent becomes about 92) and stops a quick fade being called lasting (18 percent becomes about 8). Enrolling 5,000 dogs for 3 years does the opposite for a quick fade: it is called lasting in about 86 percent of trials, although by the end of the trial only a 3 percent risk cut is left.** Three predictions were confirmed. The fourth was half right: its second number landed on the line (87 percent in our run against a predicted ceiling of 85).

This is a calculation under stated assumptions. Nothing here says any treatment works, lasts or fades. Every effect size and fading speed is an assumption, chosen to see what a trial could detect. Only the first design (580 dogs, 3 years) is the rapamycin trial's published one; the other two are our variations. The results do not question that trial's own power statement, which answers a different question (a large, lasting cut in risk).

**This run could not open the rapamycin trial's design paper** (Coleman et al. 2025). Its inputs are taken from the earlier reading recorded in this repository. The independent check could not open it either.

## What this shows

1. **"Lasting" means "still there, on average, while we watched".** The test gives one number for the whole time after dosing. The check showed that this number is close to an average of the true multiplier, weighted by when the dogs die (within about 0.01). A slow fade and a permanent benefit of the same average size give the same number.
2. **A fading benefit can look more lasting than a permanent one.** In the published design the slow fade is reported as lasting in 65 percent of trials and the permanent half-lasting benefit in 38 percent. The slow fade simply has a larger average effect inside the 3 years (ratio 0.65 against 0.75).
3. **The published design cannot tell a quick fade from a small permanent benefit.** The quick fade gives a typical ratio of 0.83 and fires in 18.5 percent of trials. A permanent 15 percent cut gives 0.85 and fires in 15.5 percent. The two look the same.
4. **More dogs sharpen the average but do not tell fading from lasting.** With 5,000 dogs the quick fade is reported as lasting in about 86 percent of trials. The answer is precise and easy to misread: the average over the 2 years after dosing was a 17 percent cut, but by the end only 3 percent was left.
5. **More years help in two ways.** Watching for 10 years lifts recognition of a real half-lasting benefit from 38 to about 92 percent, because nearly all dogs die inside the trial. It also lets a quick fade wash out of the average (reported as lasting in 7 to 8 percent, ratio 0.95). A slow fade is still reported as lasting in about 60 percent of 10-year trials, with a ratio (0.83) that is true of no single moment.
6. **The after-dosing ratio depends on how long the trial watches.** The same slow fade gives 0.65 with 3 years and 0.83 with 10. So the ratio is a property of the treatment and the trial together, not of the treatment alone.
7. **A small permanent part is missed by every design tried.** A lasting 10 percent cut is recognised in 9 percent of published-design trials, about a quarter of 10-year trials and under half of 5,000-dog trials.

**What a trial could do with this.** Say in advance that a "lasting" result means an average over the watching period. Report the after-dosing ratio together with the length of watching. Plan a look at whether the ratio drifts over time. The check tried the simplest version (separate ratios for the first and second year after dosing): it flags the quick fade in 66 percent of 5,000-dog trials and in only 14 percent of 580-dog trials, with false alarms of 2 to 3 percent for a steady benefit. So the published design has too few deaths to see a drift. These last figures are the check's own and are not in `results.json`.

## Independent check

A separate agent wrote its own simulation (death times by bisection, where ours interpolates), its own fitting code and its own log-rank test from a description of the model, before reading our code. It ran 4,000 trials per case for the 580-dog designs and 1,500 for the 5,000-dog design, with its own seeds. It tested its simulator and our formula on 400,000 dogs against direct numerical integration (agreement within 0.0006). It reran our script, and fed about 3,600 of its own simulated trials to both sets of fitting code: one decision differed. It found no coding error and no departure from `PREDICTION.md`.

All of the roughly 40 numbers it compared agreed within simulation noise, with one case worth noting:

- **D2, a fifth lasts: 24.8 percent in our run, about 22 in the check's.** Pooled over 8,000 trials each, our code gives about 24.0 and the check's about 22.1. The check traced it: the two fitting codes agree on 2,499 of 2,500 trials and the two simulators give matching results through one fit, so it reads as chance. It does not touch any prediction. We now quote "about a quarter".

What the check changed or added:

- **P4's second part is on the line, not clearly refuted.** Our run gave 87.0 percent; the check's own code gave 85.4 (1,500 trials) and our code with a new seed gave 87.3 (1,000 trials). Pooled, about 86. We score it as half and half and quote "about 86 percent".
- **P3's upper limit of 70 percent in D1 holds with a modest margin**: 65.0 in our run, 66.3 in the check's, 68.4 from our code with a new seed and 4,000 trials.
- It supplied the weighted-average reading in point 1, with its own calculation (slow fade: 0.647 for D1 and 0.839 for D2, against simulated 0.645 and 0.829).
- It supplied the split-period figures above, and points 4 and 6 in their present form.
- It reminded us that 580 dogs is the trial's target (210 enrolled at the last published count).

**Not checked at the source:** the trial's design inputs. The check tried once to open the design paper, the request was not answered, and it did not work around that. It confirmed only that our inputs match what this repository records.

## How this compares with published work

`papers/literature-check.md` records that in mice part of a benefit lasted after a treatment stopped (Hahn et al. 2019) and that in flies it vanished within days (Mair et al. 2003). Those are animal experiments with whole-life follow-up and none is in dogs. Neither was opened in this run. Our fading speeds are not taken from them.

The rule from human medicine that after-treatment effects should be estimated from the after-treatment period (Nayak et al. 2018, as recorded in the literature check) is what the two-period test does. This experiment adds a caution to it: one after-treatment number is an average, and the average depends on how long the trial watches.

The literature check did not find, in the searches run, published rates for a slowly fading or partly lasting benefit being reported as lasting in an aging-drug withdrawal trial, so there is no published figure to compare these with. That is a statement about a limited search.

## Limits

1. **Every effect size and fading speed is an assumption**, and so is the smooth shape of the fade. Nobody knows how a benefit would fade in a dog.
2. **Best case throughout.** The simulated dogs follow the doubling law exactly, nobody drops out, every owner gives the treatment, dogs do not differ in hidden ways, and all dogs are enrolled. The real trial had enrolled 210 of its planned 580 dogs at the last published count (September 2025). Real rates would be lower.
3. **D2 and D3 are not real trials.** In the 10-year design 98 percent of placebo dogs die inside the trial. A permanent cut in risk lasting 9 years after one year of dosing is a strong assumption.
4. **The doubling law overstates risk in dogs older than about 16** (experiment 004). The 10-year design follows dogs to ages 17 to 20, so its figures lean on the law where it is weakest.
5. **The split-period figures come from the check only**, with 400 to 1,500 trials, and are not part of this experiment's saved results. A proper test of that idea is queued (021, 026).
6. **1,000 trials per cell (400 for D3).** Differences of 2 to 3 percentage points between cells should not be read.
7. **ASSUMPTION: the any-difference test** is a two-sided log-rank test at the 5 percent level. The design paper is recorded as naming a Cox model.
