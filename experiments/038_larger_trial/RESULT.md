# Experiment 038: result

**A larger trial makes the trap worse, not better. When a treatment cuts risk by 60 percent in the sturdier half of dogs and 40 percent in the frailer half, and no dog ages more slowly, the hidden-differences slope test reports slowed aging in about 15 percent of simulated 10-year trials with 600 dogs, 29 percent with 1,300, 54 percent with 2,600 and about 80 percent with 5,000. The estimated rate of aging is wrong by the same amount at every size (about 0.89 where the truth is 1); more dogs only make the trial surer of the wrong figure. All four predictions were confirmed.**

This is a calculation under stated assumptions. It says how analyses behave on simulated dogs. It does not say that any treatment works, or that any real treatment helps frail or sturdy dogs more. The spread of hidden risk and the sizes of all the cuts are assumptions.

## The setting

Every dog has a hidden personal risk factor. The "sturdier half" is the half with lower hidden risk among dogs that entered at the same age. Cuts are written as "sturdier half and frailer half". Except in the rows called "true slowing", no dog's rate of aging changes, so every report of slowed or faster aging in those rows is false.

Two analyses were used, both unchanged from experiment 017. The **plain** slope test ignores hidden differences. The **hidden-differences** test (experiment 012) has one extra setting for their spread. A sound test reports slowed aging in about 2.5 percent of trials and faster aging in about 2.5 percent.

The trials: 600 to 5,000 dogs, half treated from enrolment, one size of dog, no dropout, spread of hidden risk 0.3. Ten years with entry ages 7 to 12, or 4 years with entry ages 10 to 14.

**The estimated rate does not move with the number of dogs.** Median estimated rate from the hidden-differences test in 10-year trials (1 means "no change"): 0.95 at every size for 55 and 45; 0.89 for 60 and 40; 0.90 for 42 and 18; 1.08 for 40 and 60; 0.80 for the true 20 percent slowing.

## What this means

1. **More dogs make a false finding more likely and more confident.** The error is in the estimate, not in the noise around it. A 10-year trial of 5,000 dogs would report slowed aging in about 4 trials in 5 from a 60-against-40 difference in benefit, and in about 1 in 4 from 55 against 45.
2. **Past about 1,300 to 2,600 dogs, a 10-year trial gains almost no power and keeps gaining false reports.** A true 20 percent slowing is recognised in 81 percent of trials at 1,300 dogs and 99 percent at 2,600. Over the same step, false reports from 60 and 40 go from 29 to 54 percent.
3. **Both directions and a weaker treatment behave the same way.** Frail dogs helped more (40 and 60) is reported as faster aging in about half of 5,000-dog trials by the hidden-differences test, and in nearly all of them by the plain test. Cuts of 42 and 18 behave like 60 and 40.
4. **With an equal cut, the hidden-differences test stays sound at 5,000 dogs** (6 percent flagged). So the false reports come from the unequal benefit, not from the test. The plain test does not stay sound: it reports faster aging in 60 percent of 5,000-dog trials when every dog gets the same 50 percent cut.
5. **A 4-year trial is less exposed, because it is too noisy to see much of anything.** At 60 and 40 its false reports rise slowly, from about 5 to about 15 percent. At 55 and 45 they stay near 4 to 6 percent at every size; the check estimates a slow rise from about 2.5 to about 5 percent that these runs are too small to show. Power for a true slowing rises faster: about 65 to 75 percent at 5,000 dogs.
6. **A simple rule gives the size at other trial sizes, roughly.** If the error stays fixed and the noise shrinks with the square root of the number of dogs, the 1,300-dog figure predicts the others. It was within 4 points in 17 of 18 cases. The exception is 40 and 60 at 5,000 dogs: the rule gives 60 percent and the simulations 51 (check: 53).
7. **The size of the estimate is a weak clue, not a test.** A true 20 percent slowing gives an estimated rate near 0.80. The unequal cuts tested here give 0.89 to 0.95. But experiment 033 found that a larger difference (70 and 30) gives 0.76, so the estimate alone cannot tell the two apart.

For a real trial this suggests one thing. The number of dogs is not a reason to trust a finding of slowed or faster aging that rests on death records alone. A large trial needs the outside support (a measure of frailty at entry, or evidence from something other than deaths) at least as much as a small one. This is a statement about the mathematics, not a comment on any trial's own analysis plan, which answers its own questions.

## Independent check

A separate agent wrote its own simulation and fitting code from a description of the model before opening ours. It ran every 5,000-dog case with both tests, 600 trials each, and the 4-year true slowing at 600 and 1,300 dogs. Its figures are in the table of predictions above. It then read our script and the fitting functions it uses, and found no errors in the simulation, in which half gets which cut, in the counting or in the seeds. It confirmed the two UK inputs (level 0.004967 a year and slope 0.3195 a year) against `dog_lifespan/results.json`, and the six 1,300-dog figures against the earlier experiments' result files.

Nine of its eleven 5,000-dog comparisons were within 2 standard errors of ours. Two were not: the 4-year true slowing (64.8 percent against our 75.3) and 42 and 18 (75.2 against 81.7). It traced the first by running both codes on the same 40 simulated trials. The plain test's figures matched to six decimal places. Its own hidden-differences fits stopped slightly short of the best fit in nearly every trial, which lowers its figures by a few points. It judged ours the better ones.

The check did not re-run the 10-year cases at 600 and 2,600 dogs or the 4-year cases below 5,000 dogs (apart from the true slowing). It did not read the likelihood code of experiments 012 and 017, which earlier checks have read.

## What the check changed

- **Two figures are stated as ranges.** 42 and 18 at 5,000 dogs: about 75 to 82 percent. The 4-year true slowing at 5,000 dogs: about 65 to 75 percent, with ours (75) the better supported.
- **"Does not grow" was softened.** We had drafted that false reports at 55 and 45 over 4 years do not grow with the number of dogs. The check measured the error there (about a third of the noise at 5,000 dogs) and showed that a slow rise from about 2.5 to about 5 percent is expected and fits our figures. Point 5 now says so.
- **The square-root rule is called rough.** The check found it over-predicts 40 and 60 at 5,000 dogs by 7 to 9 points. Point 6 says "roughly" and names the exception.
- **Labels added.** The trial lengths and "no dropout" are now labelled as assumptions in the script.
- The check also found that with an equal 50 percent cut over 4 years the hidden-differences test's median estimate is 1.01, so the test itself has no lean there, while the plain test's is 1.16.

## Comparison with published work

`papers/literature-check.md` names no published work on how a benefit that differs between individuals affects a test for a change in the rate of aging, or on how that changes with the size of a trial, and none was found in the searches run for it. It credits the idea of hidden frailty, and of survivors becoming a sturdier selection over time, to Beard (1959) and Vaupel, Manton and Stallard (1979); the trap measured here is that mechanism acting on the treated group. That a larger sample makes a test more certain of an estimate that is off is a general, well-known property of statistics, not a finding of this project. No new search was run, because web pages could not be opened in this run (Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 were tried again on 20 November 2026 and could not be opened).

## Limits

1. Everything is assumed: the spread of hidden risk (0.3 has no measurement behind it), the sizes of the cuts, and the two-halves pattern. Nobody has measured whether any treatment helps frail or sturdy dogs more.
2. One spread (0.3), one size of dog, no dropout, two trial designs. The entry ages and trial lengths are assumptions and are not those of any running trial.
3. 300 trials per case at 2,600 and 5,000 dogs: those figures are uncertain by up to 3 points. Only the 5,000-dog cases were re-run by the check.
4. Only small differences (55 and 45, 60 and 40) were run over 4 years. Experiment 033 found 23 percent false reports at 70 and 30 with 1,300 dogs; larger 4-year trials with that difference were not run.
5. The square-root rule was tested on six patterns only and misses one of them at 5,000 dogs.
6. No way of telling a true slowing from an unequal benefit using death records alone was tested here.
