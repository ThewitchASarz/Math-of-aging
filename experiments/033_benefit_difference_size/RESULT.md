# Experiment 033: result

**A modest difference in benefit is enough to mislead a slope test. In simulated 10-year trials of 1,300 dogs, a treatment that cuts risk by 60 percent in the sturdier half of dogs and 40 percent in the frailer half is reported as slowed aging in about 29 percent of trials by the hidden-differences test, though no dog ages more slowly. A 55-against-45 difference gives about 10 percent. The plain test tolerates more, because it leans the other way to begin with: about 3 percent at 60 and 40, about 24 percent at 65 and 35. A narrower spread of hidden risk does not make the trap go away, a benefit that varies steadily is worse than two halves, and a 4-year trial is not immune.** Two predictions were confirmed, one was half right and one was refuted.

This is a calculation under stated assumptions. It says how analyses behave on simulated dogs. It does not say that any treatment works, or that any real treatment helps sturdy dogs more than frail ones. The spread of hidden risk, the sizes of the cuts and both benefit patterns are assumptions.

## The setting

Every dog has a hidden personal risk factor. A dog's "rank" is its place in hidden risk among dogs that entered at the same age. The treatment cuts risk by 50 percent on average in every case. In no case does any dog's rate of aging change. So every report of slowed aging below is false.

Two benefit patterns were used. In "two halves", the sturdier half of dogs gets one cut and the frailer half another. In "steady", the cut falls in a straight line with rank, from the sturdiest dog to the frailest.

Two analyses were used, both unchanged from experiment 017. The **plain** slope test ignores hidden differences. The **hidden-differences** test (experiment 012) has one extra setting for their spread. A sound test reports slowed aging in about 2.5 percent of trials.

## What this means

1. **The smallest difference that matters is small.** For the hidden-differences test, false reports of slowed aging pass 10 percent at about 55 against 45, and reach 29 percent at 60 against 40. For the plain test they pass 10 percent somewhere between 60 against 40 and 65 against 35.
2. **The plain test looks sturdier only because two errors cancel.** With an equal cut for every dog and a spread of 0.3, the plain test leans towards "faster aging" (it flags about one trial in five that way; this is the fault experiment 012 repaired). A benefit that favours sturdy dogs pushes the other way. At 60 against 40 the two roughly cancel. That is luck, not protection: with the cuts the other way round the two errors add (experiment 017 found 78 percent flagged as faster aging at 70 and 30).
3. **A narrow spread of hidden risk does not remove the trap. This prediction was wrong.** We expected a spread of 0.1 to weaken the trap. At 70 and 30 it did not: the plain test reported slowed aging in 66 percent of trials at spread 0.1, against 55 percent at spread 0.3, and the hidden-differences test in 80 percent against 87. The trap does not need dogs to differ much to begin with. The treatment itself makes them differ, by protecting some dogs much more than others. The plain test does worse at the narrow spread because its lean towards "faster aging" is weaker there, so less of the error is cancelled. The check added that slightly more dogs die at the narrow spread (94 against 90 percent), which sharpens the test.
4. **A steady pattern is worse than two halves, not safer.** With the same half averages as 60 and 40, a cut that falls steadily from 70 to 30 percent gave 46 percent false reports, against 29 for two halves. The steady pattern reaches further at both ends.
5. **A 4-year trial is not immune. This prediction was wrong.** We expected few false reports in a 4-year trial. At 70 and 30 the plain test gave 12 percent and the hidden-differences test 23 percent. That is the same size as the chance that such a trial recognises a true 20 percent slowing (23 to 26 percent in experiment 017). So in a 4-year trial, a large difference in benefit and a true slowing of aging are about equally likely to produce a "slowed aging" finding.
6. **Sizes and dropout weaken the trap but leave it large.** With five size classes and 5 percent dropout a year, 70 and 30 gave 31 and 62 percent, against 55 and 87 without. Fewer dogs die in that design (64 percent against 90), so every slope finding, true or false, is rarer. Both figures were below what we predicted.

For a real trial this suggests: a finding of slowed aging from death records alone cannot be told apart from a treatment that helps sturdier dogs somewhat more, even when the difference is as modest as 60 against 40 percent. Experiments 032 and 034 found that a frailty score does not repair this unless it is nearly perfect, but that it can warn. So a slope finding needs support from something other than the death records. This is a statement about the mathematics, not a comment on any trial's own analysis plan, which answers its own questions.

## Independent check

A separate agent wrote its own simulation and fitting code from a description of the model, with its own random draws, before opening ours. It ran all ten one-size cases with both tests, 600 trials each. Its figures are in the table of predictions above. All 20 comparisons were within 1.6 standard errors of ours.

It then read our code and the functions it imports from experiments 001, 012 and 017, and found no errors in the simulation of hidden risk and rank, the benefit patterns (each averages a 50 percent cut), the death times, the counting or the seeds. It confirmed the two UK inputs (level 0.004967 a year and slope 0.3195 a year) against `dog_lifespan/results.json`, and the size-class rates and shares against the files in `dog_lifespan/data/`.

It did not re-run the sizes-and-dropout case; it reviewed the code for it. So the two figures for that case rest on our run alone.

## What the check changed

- **One reference figure is stated from the check, not from our run.** With an equal 50 percent cut, our 600 trials had the plain test flagging faster aging in 25.3 percent. Experiment 012 had 18.9 percent in 800 trials. The check ran 4,600 trials and found about 21 percent. Our 25.3 was a high chance draw. The tables above say "about 21 percent". `results.json` keeps the 25.3 that the script produced.
- **The explanation in point 3 was tested, and widened.** The check confirmed the surprise at spread 0.1 in separate runs of 1,200 trials (67 against 55 percent). It measured the plain test's lean with an equal cut at both spreads (median estimated rate 1.10 at spread 0.3 and 1.03 at 0.1). It also pointed out that the median estimated rate at 70 and 30 is nearly the same at the two spreads (0.81 and 0.82), so the lean is not the whole story; more deaths at the narrow spread also play a part. How much each contributes was not separated.
- **A limit was added** (number 3 below) about the sizes-and-dropout design.
- No claim was withdrawn.

## Comparison with published work

`papers/literature-check.md` names no published work on how a benefit that differs between individuals affects a test for a change in the rate of aging, and none was found in the searches run for it. It credits the idea of hidden frailty, and of survivors becoming a sturdier selection over time, to Beard (1959) and Vaupel, Manton and Stallard (1979); the trap measured here is that mechanism acting on the treated group. No new search was run, because web pages could not be opened in this run (Raz et al. 2026 and McMillan et al. 2024 were tried again on 19 November 2026 and could not be opened).

## Limits

1. Everything is assumed: the spread of hidden risk (0.3 has no measurement behind it; 0.1 is about the size fitted to the UK table in experiment 004), the sizes of the cuts, and both patterns. Nobody has measured whether any treatment helps sturdy dogs more.
2. The average cut is 50 percent throughout. A smaller average cut with the same ratio between the halves was not tested.
3. The sizes-and-dropout design is experiment 017's. Its size-class settings come from a calibration that also had a constant background death rate, which the simulation leaves out, so fewer dogs die in it (64 percent) than in the one-size design (90 percent). Its figures should not be compared closely with the one-size figures. The same spread is assumed inside every size class.
4. Only sturdy dogs helped more was tested here. The opposite direction (read as faster aging) was measured in experiment 017 at 70 and 30 only.
5. One trial size (1,300 dogs). The error is in the estimate itself, so a larger trial is expected to give more false reports, not fewer; this was not tested.
6. 600 trials per case: each figure is uncertain by 1 to 2 points, and one reference figure was a high chance draw (see "What the check changed").
7. "The threshold" depends on the test, the spread and the pattern. The 10 percent line is a convenient mark, not a rule.
