# Experiment 036: result

**Grouping the frailty score in both arms repairs the grouped warning test. With a treatment that does nothing, the five-groups test fires in about 5 to 6 percent of simulated trials for every score tried, where the treated-only version fired in 16 percent. But the grouped test is the weaker warning when the benefit rises or falls steadily with the score: with sturdy dogs helped more and a score of quality 0.7 it notices in about 72 percent of trials against about 88 percent for the straight-line test. It earns its place only for a pattern with no steady trend. When the middle dogs are helped most, the straight-line test is nearly blind (about 5 to 17 percent) while the grouped test notices in every trial with a perfect score. With a score of quality 0.7, though, even the grouped test sees that pattern in only about 26 percent of trials.** All four predictions were confirmed.

This is a calculation under stated assumptions. It says how analyses behave on simulated dogs. It does not say that any treatment works, that any treatment helps some dogs more, or that any real frailty measurement reaches any of these qualities. The spread of hidden risk (0.3), the form of the scores and every size and pattern of benefit are assumptions. No dog's rate of aging changes in any case.

## The four tests

Each asks one thing: does the treatment's cut in risk differ between dogs with different frailty scores? None of them allows a change in the rate of aging. Entry age has its own setting in all of them.

- **Straight-line**: the cut may change in a straight line with the score. One extra setting.
- **Five groups, treated only**: a separate cut for each fifth of the score, with untreated dogs held to a straight line. Experiment 034's faulty version, kept for comparison.
- **Five groups, both arms**: a separate cut for each fifth, and a separate risk level for each fifth in both arms.
- **Three groups, both arms**: the same with thirds.

The **rank score** is the dog's rank in hidden risk among dogs of the same entry age, read with error. The **risk score** is the dog's hidden risk itself, read with error. Quality runs from 0 (useless) to 1 (perfect).

## What this means

1. **The repair suggested by experiment 034's check holds.** Give each fifth of the score its own risk level in both arms, and the grouped test fires in about 5 to 6 percent of trials when the treatment does nothing. That is true for both kinds of score and all qualities tried.
2. **The treated-only version is faulty more widely than experiment 034 saw.** It fires too often not only with a perfect rank score (16 percent) but also at rank quality 0.9 (11 percent) and with the risk score at quality 0.7 (about 10 percent). It should not be used.
3. **An equal benefit is not read as an unequal one.** With risk cut by 50 percent in every dog, the sound tests fire in about 4 to 7 percent of trials. Hidden differences the score does not see do not make the warning cry wolf to any large degree. The check's larger runs suggest the true figure is a little above 5 (see below).
4. **For a steady pattern the straight-line test is the better warning.** With sturdy dogs helped more and a score of quality 0.7 it notices in about 88 percent of trials; the five-group test in about 72 percent; the three-group test in about 77 percent. At quality 0.5 the gap is wider: 54 against 30 percent. With the smaller difference (60 and 40) at quality 0.7, the figures are about 36 and 20 percent.
5. **For a pattern with no steady trend the grouped test is the only one that sees it, and only with a good score.** When the middle dogs are helped most, the straight-line test fires in about 5 to 17 percent of trials whatever the score. The five-group test notices in 100 percent with a perfect score and about 90 percent at quality 0.9, but only about 26 to 30 percent at quality 0.7 and about 10 percent at 0.5.
6. **Not predicted: three groups against five.** Three groups do a little better than five for a steady pattern (77 against 72 percent at quality 0.7) and worse for the middle pattern at quality 0.9 (80 against 90 percent). Neither number of groups is better across the board.
7. **A silent warning test is not a clean bill of health.** At score quality 0.7 a real difference in benefit is often missed. With cuts of 70 and 30 percent the straight-line test misses it in about 1 trial in 8 and the five-group test in more than 1 in 4. With cuts of 60 and 40 percent the straight-line test misses it in about 2 trials in 3 and the five-group test in about 4 in 5. Experiments 033 and 038 found that a 60 and 40 split is already enough to make a slope test report slowed aging that is not there.

For a real trial this suggests: if the analysis asks whether the benefit differs by a frailty score, the straight-line test is the natural main test, and a grouped test (grouped in both arms) is a useful second look for patterns that are not steady. Running both means two chances of a false alarm, so the pair fires more often than 5 percent when nothing is there; this experiment did not measure by how much. This is a statement about the mathematics, not a comment on any trial's own analysis plan, which answers its own questions.

## Independent check

A separate agent wrote its own simulation and fitting code from a description of the model, with its own random draws, before opening anything in this folder. It ran eleven cases with 1,500 to 2,500 trials each. Its figures are in the tables above. Of 44 comparisons with ours, 43 were within 2 standard errors and one was at 2.0 (the treated-only test with sturdy dogs helped more at rank quality 0.5: 29.1 percent against our 33.7; that figure is not in the tables above). With 44 comparisons, one at that distance is expected by chance.

It then read our code and the functions it imports. It found no errors in the simulation, the two patterns of benefit, the scores, the likelihood, the groups, the number of extra settings or the counting. It confirmed that no fitted setting was near its limit in a sample of 90 fits, and that the larger model never fitted worse than the smaller one. It confirmed the two inputs (level 0.004967 a year and slope 0.3195 a year) against `dog_lifespan/results.json`. It noted that our code does not test whether each fit reports success; its own sample of our fits had all converged.

It did not re-run the rank score at quality 0.9, or the risk score outside the no-effect and equal-cut cases.

## What the check changed

- **"About 5 percent" is now "about 5 to 6 percent".** With more trials than ours, the check found the sound tests slightly above 5 percent when nothing differs: the straight-line test at 6.3 to 7.3 percent (equal cut, quality 0.7) and 6.4 to 6.8 percent (no effect, rank score), and the five-group test at up to 6.6 percent. Our 600-trial figures (4 to 7 percent) agree within chance. The likely reason, in the check's view: none of these analyses has a setting for hidden differences between dogs, so they are fitted to data they do not quite describe. The excess is small. It is recorded as an open issue.
- **Point 5 was narrowed.** A first draft said only the grouped test sees the middle pattern. The check pointed out that at quality 0.7 the grouped test misses it in most trials, and the straight-line test fires a little above its false-alarm rate (10 to 17 percent). The result now says so.
- **Point 7 was added** at the check's suggestion: a test that does not fire does not show the benefit is equal.
- No claim was withdrawn.

## Comparison with published work

`papers/literature-check.md` names no published work on testing whether a benefit differs by a frailty score in a dog trial, and none was found in the searches run for it. Testing for a treatment effect that differs between groups, and the loss of sharpness when a continuous measurement is cut into groups, are standard topics in trial statistics; no source for them was opened in this run, so none is cited. No new search was run in this run, and the two papers that queue items 022 and 023 wait on (Raz et al. 2026, McMillan et al. 2024) were not tried again.

## Limits

1. Everything is assumed: the spread of hidden risk (0.3; nobody has measured it for dogs), every benefit size and pattern, and the form of both scores.
2. The true split in the "halves" cases falls at the middle of the ranking, inside the middle fifth and the middle third. The "middle" pattern's edges fall at the quarters, which no fifth or third matches. Other patterns or numbers of groups could give different figures.
3. Only the 10-year, 1,300-dog trial with one size of dog and no dropout was tested.
4. The score is measured once, at entry, with plain bell-curve error.
5. 600 trials per case: each figure is uncertain by 1 to 2 points. The same simulated dogs are used for all six scores within a case, so rows of a table are not independent.
6. No analysis here has a setting for hidden differences. Whether adding one brings the false alarms from about 6 back to 5 percent was not tested.
7. The false-alarm rate of running the straight-line and grouped tests together was not measured.
